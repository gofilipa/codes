1. the current data grab

We are in the midst of an intense acceleration of what Ulises A. Meijas and Nick Couldry call a "data grab." The data grab is the heir of colonialism's "land grab," the long process over several centuries in which Europe, despite representing 8 percent of the world's landmass, seized over 84 percent of its total territory (Mejias & Couldry 5). According to Mejias and Couldry, this data grab took traditional colonial processes of exploration, extraction, and exploitation of resources and applied them to a new object, to data. "[B]oth systems," they explain, "are founded on an appropriation of the world’s resources, treating them as ‘just there’ for the taking. Historically it was the territories… In the contemporary era, it is human life, extracted in the form of data" (Mejias & Couldry 18).

The object, and particularly its digital nature, required new strategies for capture that differ from those of land-based resources. First, rather than partition zones of extraction in the form of land territories, there has emerged the digital platform. The platform is a digital space, which can range in scope from individual websites on the internet to self-enclosed apps, which contains mechanisms that gather up details of the user's identity and activity—not only that which has been voluntarily given, but also that contained in hidden codes and protocols that append all data traveling over the internet. In more technical terms, the platform is a server location, from which the user requests data (via the HTTP protocol), and in that request, also sends some information to the server. Most recently, the best way of tracking users is in the form of a "cookie," a bit of data which the server assigns to the user and which can be used to track that user's activity on that and other servers. (Although, cookies can also be manipulated by users to simulate real web requests that can bypass web blockers, as I demonstrate at the end of the chapter).

If the first strategy takes advantage of technical protocols, the second strategy takes advantage of legal ones. Besides new procedures for capture, it is necessry also to create the proper pretense for it. In traditional colonialism, Mejias and Couldry explain, this pretense was explicitly coercive. They offer the example of the Requerimiento, a document that the Spanish colonizers read to the indigenous peoples of Americas as they entered their land and villages. The document certified the Spanish authority, in the name of the king and the church, to take the land, its resources and people, under Spanish control. It declared all who heard it subject to the crown, and any those who resisted to be violently subdued. Mejias and Couldry compare this document to the "Terms of Service" agreement from Google Chrome, which also makes claims upon the user, in particular on the "Content" that a user inputs into the system. The 2007 version of the TOS asserts that,

You give Google a perpetual, irrevocable, worldwide, royalty-free, and non-exclusive license to reproduce, adapt, modify, translate, publish, publicly perform, publicly display and distribute any Content which you [the user] submit, post or display on or through, the Services. (Rpt. in Mejias & Couldry 12).

More recently, the wording of TOS has muted the explictness of this language. Recent wordings of Google TOS, the authors point out, don't need to be as assertive: "without most of us ever having read those original terms and conditions…. the extraction of data is already well underway" (Mejias & Couldry 12). The more recent strategy has shifted from assertive to excessive and at times obscure language (these TOS now occupy many pages). For example, the 2024 Google TOS, which is now held on its own subdomain, https://policies.google.com, phrases like "operating and improving the services" and "to customize our services for you" offer the necessary cover for "using automated systems and algorithms to analyze your content" ("License", "Google Privacy and Terms"). Additionally, the TOS contains strange and difficult to parse formulations, like "We need your permission if your intellectual property rights restrict our use of your content. You provide Google with that permission through this license" ("License", "Google Privacy and Terms"). The meaning appears to be that Google will need, yet already has obtained, permission from the user. But what exactly is one giving permission for Google to do? The TOS does not say.

That is if one even bothers to read the terms of service. Whereas the Requerimiento relied on blunt language claiming an authority over land and people, more recent TOS rely on the user not even checking the terms. While these approaches, separated by centuries, differ dramatically, they both rely on the user not knowing the extent of the rights being taken from them. The Requerimiento, similar to modern-day TOS, used a language that was obscure to its audience. As Mejias and Couldry point out, "[the conquerors] read this document in Spanish… to indigenous or First Nation peoples who did not speak Spanish!" (13). The legal strategy, then, maintains this dependence on a certain level of ignorance from the user.

Like land, the data of the world will be taken until it meets sufficient resistance to stop it. Shoshana Zuboff, whose 2015 essay ""Big Other: Surveillance Capitalism and the Prospects of an Information Civilization" (and followup 2018 book, The Age of Surveillance Capitalism: The Fight for a Human Future at the New Frontier of Power) helped to formalize the notion of capture by the captial forces, what Zuboff calls the "logic of accumulation" (78).1 Her essay analyzes papers written by Google’s Chief Economist Hal Varian, where he discusses the potential of "computer-mediated economic transactions" (Rpt. Zuboff 78). Varian identifies four new kinds of business activities made possible by computation, which are "data extraction and analysis," "new contractual forms," "personalization and customization," and "continuous experiments" (Zuboff 78). Most of these rely on the nearly infinite streams of data that platforms can capture from users, and on the fact that these streams are mostly hidden from view, thus creating asymmetrical relationships between the consumer and the business, which obviates the role of trust. As Zuboff explains, "New possibilities of subjugation are produced as this innovative institutional logic thrives on unexpected and illegible mechanisms of extraction and control that exile persons from their own behavior" (85).

This shift from "market" to "surveillance" capitalism, however, is not as neat as Zuboff would have it. As critics like Simone Brown and Ruha Benjamin point out, surveillance has been happening to Black people for centuries. Brown's work surfaces the quantifying and policing practices that Black people have endured since the slave ships, while Benjamin's explores the ways that new technologies perpetuate old discriminations under the guise technical values like "neutrality." Benjamin points out that "Far from coming upon a sinister story of racist programmers scheming in the dark corners of the web, we will find that the desire for objectivity, efficiency, profitability, and progress fuels the pursuit of technical fixes across many different social arenas" (7). For some groups of people, surveillance has always been a reigning paradigm.

Yet, the extention of tracking into digital realms does bring something new, which Zuboff shows in her concept of "informate."

Despite that, her method of close reading business documents is valuable.
