*** REVISE paranoid & reparative reading
**** paranoid reading
Paranoid reading is an affective stance that structures critical
lanalysis. Paranoia is first defined by Sedgwick in her introduction
to a 1997 book of essays, /Novel Gazing: Queer Readings of Fiction/.
In the introduction, Sedgwick explains that the essays "reflect a
distance from those master-figures or master-discourses," by which she
means New Historicist work that is directly indebted to "Marx,
Nietzsche, and Freud" (/Novel Gazing/ 2). Critical indebted to these
writers, pursues a logic of demystification and exposure, which seeks
to reveal the hidden realities of oppression. what Sedgwick calls. "In
the context of recent U.S. critical theory," Sedgwick explains, "to
apply a hermeneutic of suspicion" is, I believe, widely understood as
a mandatory injunction rather than a possibility among other
possibilities" (/Novel Gazing/ 3).

There are five qualities that define paranoia, according to Sedgwick.
Paranoia:

- is anticipatory. It predicts and preempts every possibility, as
  Sedgwick says, "The first imperative of paranoia is '/There must be
  no bad surprises/'" (emphasis original; /Novel Gazing/ 9). Rather
  than accept unfavorable possibilities, according to the
  "future-oriented vigilance [that] paranoia generates... bad news
  [must] be always already known" (/Novel Gazing/ 10).
- is reflexive, mimetic. Paranoia reproduces itself, propagating and
  spreading like a virus from host to host. Sedgwick explains that
  "Paranoia seems to require to be imitated in order to be
  understood... '/Anything you can do, I can do worse/' and '/Anything
  you can do, I can do first/'" (emphasis original /Novel Gazing/ 10).
  One effect is that it creates self-reinforcing oppositional
  relationships, such as Foucault's posing of repressive hypothesis,
  which leads to a self-perpetuating narrative of hegemony vs
  subversion that, according to Sedgwick "propagat[es] the repressive
  hypothesis ever more broadly by means of displacement,
  multiplication, hypostatization" (/Touching Feeling/ 10).
- is strong theory - it invades, consumes all phenomena into its
  worldview. This quality gives paranoia "reach and reductiveness,"
  which makes "tautological thinking hard to identify, even as it
  makes it compelling and near-inevitable" (/Novel Gazing/ 13, 15).
  For example, referring to D.A. Miller's book, /The Novel and the
  Police/, a study of surveillance, policing, and disciplinary culture
  in Victorian novels, Sedgwick points out that "the main argument or
  'strong theory' of /The Novel and the Police/ is entirely circular:
  everything can be understood as an aspect of the carceral, therefore
  the carceral is everywhere" (/Novel Gazing/ 14).
- is negative theory - paranoia is driven by the minimization of
  negative affect rather than the maximization of positive affect,
  "the anticipation of pain in one case, [against] the provision of
  pleasure in the other" (/Novel Gazing/ 16). Sedgwick poses Silvan
  Thompkin and Melanie Klein's positive affect theories against
  "paranoid Freudian epistemology," where "it is implausible enough to
  suppose then that truth could be even an accidental occasion of joy;
  inconceivable to imagine joy as a guarantor of truth" (/Novel
  Gazing/ 16). 
- follows a narrative of exposure - its obssesive goal is to unearth,
  surface, reveal some hidden reality (often of oppression or
  injustice) as knowledge. Sedgwick explains with a compelling image:
  "Like the deinstitutionalized person on the street who, betrayed and
  plotted against by everyone else in the city, still urges on you the
  finger-worn dossier bristling with his precious correspondence,
  paranoia for all its vaunted suspicion acts as though its work would
  be accomplished if only it could finally, this time, somehow get its
  story truly known" (/Novel Gazing/ 17). It is a faith in knowledge
  which, as Sedgwick points out, can be in reality entirely impotent
  ("for someone to have an unmystified, angry view of large and
  genuinely systemic oppressions does not /instrinsically/ or
  /necessarily/ enjoin on that person any specific train of
  epistemological or narrative consequences" (/Novel Gazing/ 4).

For Sedgwick, paranoid reading is a crucial framework for analysis,
allowing much important work to happen. She refers to her own work and
how it has been driven by paranoia. For example, her field-defining
work, /The Epistemology of the Closet/, is a study in hidden
sexualities, and the power structures they maintain, in literature.
Sedgwick's agenda in the book is to expose the dependence of a
privileged heterosexual position upon the existence of a subordinated
homosexual. How do these sexual categories and closet which they
structure appear in 19th-20th century literature? For example, her
chapter on Oscar Wilde's /The Picture of Dorian Gray/ examines
abstraction and figuration as strategies for encoding male on male
desire which otherwise couldn't be expressed: "the modernist impulse
toward abstraction in the first place owes an incalculable part of its
energy precisely to the turn-of-the-century male homo/hetero
definitional panic... the 'figuration' that had to be abjected from
modernist self-reflexive abstraction was not the figuration of
figurality itself, but, rather, that represented in a very particular
body, the desired male body" (/Epistemology/, 167). This figuration I
turn to in my third chapter.[fn:16]

Nonetheless, these structures are unhelpful for thought, even "a
cognitive danger... a moralisitic tautology that became increasingly
incabable of recognizing itself as such" (/Touching Feeling/ 12).
Reading Foucault's /History of Sexuality, Vol. 1/, Sedgwick says, "I
knew what I wanted from it: some ways of understanding human desire
that might be quite to the side of prohibition and repression, that
might hence be structured quite differently from the heroic,
'liberatory', inescapably dualistic righteousness of hunting down and
attacking prohibition/repression in all its chameleonic guises"
(/Touching Feeling/ 10).

**** reparative reading
She thus pursues a critical method outside the paranoid logic, which
she calls "reparative reading." In contrast to paranoid reading,
reparative reading prioritizes pleasure, surprise, and hope:
#+begin_quote
to read from a reparative position is to surrender the knowing,
anxious paranoid determination that no horror, however apparently
unthinkable, shall ever come to the reader as new: to a reparatively
positioned reader, it can seem realistic and necessary to experience
surprise. Because there can be terrible surprises, however, there can
also be good ones. Hope, often a fracturing, even a traumatic thing to
experience, is among the energies by which the reparatively positioned
reader tries to organize the fragments and part-objects she encounters
or creates. Because she has room to realize that the future may be
different from the present, it is also possible for her to entertain
such profoundly painful, profoundly relieving, ethically crucial
possibilities as that the past, in tum, could have happened
differently from the way it actually did. (/Novel Gazing 23-24)
#+end_quote
Reparative motives do not exist in a vacuum from paranoia. Rather, the
both often work together: "Powerful reparative practices... infuse
self-avowedly paranoid critical projects" (/Novel Gazing/ 8). However,
a reparative recognizes most of all "that the culture surrounding it
is inadequate or inimical to its nurture" (/Novel Gazing/ 28). In
response, Sedgwick argues, is "additive and accretive... it wants to
assemble and confer plentitude on an object that will then have
resources to offer an inchoate self" (/Novel Gazing/ 26-27).

Close reading is the tool par excelance of reparative work.
Particularly, Sedgwick stipulates a kind of "unhurried, undefensive,
theoretically galvanized practice of close reading," one which
permeates the essays in /Novel Gazing/. These essays, according to
Sedgwick, approach criticism in "a much more speculative,
superstitious, and methodologically adventurous state where
recognitions, pleasures, and discoveries seep in only from the most
stretched and ragged edges of one's competence" (/Novel Gazing/ 3).

#+begin_quote
Often these readings begin from or move toward sites of same-sex,
interpersonal eroticism---but not necessarily so. It seems to me that
an often quiet, but very palpable presiding image here---a kind of
/genius loci/ for queer reading---is the interpretive absorption of
the child or adolescent whose sense of personal queerness may or may
not (yet?) have resolved into a sexual specificity of proscribed
object choice, aim, site, or identification. Such a child---if she
reads at all---is reading for important news about herself, without
knowing what form that news will take; with only the patchiest
familiarity with its codes; without, even, more than hungrily
hypothesizing to what questions this news may proffer an answer.
/Novel Gazing/ 2-3
#+end_quote

Years later, Sedgwick will perform her own reparative reading of
shame, an affect which is typically a negative, structuring force in
queer identity. In the essay entitled "Shame, Theatricality, Queer
Performativity," from her collection of essays in /Touching Feeling/,
Sedgwick examines the writings of Henry James for evidence of shame.
However, rather than plumb shame's depths for what it reveals about a
hidden sexuality, Sedgwick explores how it might function as a
generative resource for creativity. She uses shame, which she
associates with words like "blusing" and "flushing," it to pull other
affects and images into relation. For example, when reflecting on his
writing practice, James,
#+begin_quote
describes his revision of the early fictions both as his (or their?)
way of "remaining /unshamed/" and as a process by which they have "all
joyously and /blushingly/ renewed themselves" (345; emphasis added).
What James seems to want here is to remove the blush from its terminal
place as the betraying blazon of a ruptured narcissistic circuit, and
instead to put it in circulation: as the sign of a tenderly
strengthened and indeed now "irresistible" bond between the writer of
the present and the abashed writer of the past, or between either of
them and the queer little /conceptus/. (emphasis original; /Touching
Feeling/ 41)
#+end_quote
Using shame as a connective tissue, Sedgwick moves from "blushing" and
"flushing" to other associated terms, like "fond." This term, which
Sedgwick describes as "one of James’s most cherished words," "marks
the place of the author’s pleasure in dramatizing himself as all but
flooded with self-absorbed delusion and embarrassment, but equally
with pleasure" (/Touching Feeling/ 55). From James's /fondness/ with
his self-embarassment, Sedgwick also reads a fixation with anal
metaphors---for, as Sedgwick points out, "fond is also the French word
for bottom, [which] may explain its affinity with [other terms like]
"retrospect," the "backward view," even with the "thrilling tale"
(/Touching Feeling/ 55). Moving from blushing to fond, then, enables
Sedgwick to discover "a node where the theatrics of shame, affection,
and display are brought together with a compositional principle and at
the same time lodged firmly, at the level of the signifier, in a
particular zone of the eroticized body" /Touching Feeling/ 56).

*** DRAFT the protocol of capture
**** summary
Text capture is the process in which text data is extracted from web
servers.

This kind of capture follows the same protocols as paranoia, driven by
a totalizing impulse which seeks to incorporate everything that it
can.

There are five central principles to text capture, which map loosely
to Sedgwick's principles of paranoia. Capture, in turn,

**** 1) /always wants more/.
If paranoia is anticipatory, capture is covetous. It is propelled by a
seeming necessity to aggregate more and more resources into its hold.

Ulises A. Meijas and Nick Couldry describe the acceleration of of
electronic information harvesting as a "data grab." They compare it to
traditional colonialism's "land grab," the long process over several
centuries where Europe, despite representing 8 percent of the world's
landmass, seized over 84 percent of its territory (Mejias & Couldry
5). The data grab applies colonial protocols of exploration,
extraction, and exploitation of resources to data. Data, like land, is
available for claim and conquest. As Couldry and Mejias explain,
"[B]oth systems are founded on an appropriation of the world’s
resources, treating them as ‘just there’ for the taking. Historically
it was the territories... In the contemporary era, it is human life,
extracted in the form of data" (Mejias & Couldry 18).

Just as colonizers allocated lands not represented in their European
maps for the Crown, so now tech companies take the text, image, videos
from the websites as unclaimed resources. According to Cloudflare, an
internet company, AI-powered web scraping grew 18% between 2024 -
2025, with Google's "Googlebot" maintaining its prominence as the
largest web scraper, while the highest gains going to "GPTBot" and
"META-ExternalAgent," which "highlight[s] the increasing prominence of
OpenAI and Meta in this category."[fn:14]

More data means more training examples, which results in
higher-quality systems that make better predictions.[fn:13] AI Ethics
researchers, Gael Varoquaux, Alexandra Sasha Luccioni and Meredith
Whittaker,[fn:10] describe this phenomenon as the "bigger-is-better
paradigm" which drives ML development.

Their research builds on a prescient paper, released four years before
(and the year before ChatGPT came out),[fn:11] "The Dangers of
Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big?🦜" (2021), which
posed the problem of sustainability with regard to scale, discussed
more in point #3 below. 

Since "Stochastic Parrots," the models have increased exponentially in
size, exacerbating the problems of environmental and data
sustainability, to a point where they are even plateauing in
performance (a point addressed in principle #3, that capture
diminishes resources and degrades quality).

***** the ouroborous of more: an industry perspective

According to Cloudflare, a major tech provider for cloud and cyber
computing services, AI crawlers increased sharply, ranging between
18%-48% in the months between May 2024 and May 2025. From this time
period, the highest increases came from OpenAI, Claude, and Google's
AI crawlers.[fn:5]

This situation has created both concern and excitement. As researcher
Ramya Chandrasekhar points out, “certain well-resourced actors like
Big Tech are able to take advantage of data flows on the open web to
internet to train proprietary foundation models, while giving little
to no value back to either the maintenance of shared informational
resources or communities of commoners" (Chandrasekhar 2).[fn:6]

Meanwhile, on the side of industry specialists, like the company Zyte,
the outlook is positve. The company's annual report embraces the
increasing of web scraping, even describing it in inevitable terms: 
#+begin_quote
For years, we have talked about the "cat-and-mouse" between data-
gatherers and data owners. But 2025 marked a significant transition in
this space. The pace of updates from bot management vendors has
accelerated, and the old methods of maximizing data success are
becoming less effective.

This is a good thing.

The complexity of modern anti-bot measures has gone past the point
where human developers can, or should, manage it manually.

...

The friction of the past 15 years is giving way to a future where data
extraction is seamless, compliant, and powered by intelligent agents.
(5-6) [fn:7]
#+end_quote

For this, and undoubtedly many, companies, the increasing automation
is an opportunity to ramp up production. The confident, non-nuanced
tone of this report, with turns of phrase like "This is a good thing,"
and "can, or should", is reminiscent of text generated by an LLM. And
why shouldn't it be?

Indeed, AI and data appear to feed one another other. In this case,
the company focused in gathering data is pushing the AI tools as an
efficiency-enhancing component. In turn, the data which is scraped is
used to train more models. The data that is scraped by AI will be fed
to AI---a kind of digital ouroboros.

***** From the Requerimiento to the TOS.
Besides new procedures
for capture, it is necessry also to create the proper pretense for it.
In traditional colonialism, Mejias and Couldry explain, this pretense
was explicitly coercive. They offer the example of the
/Requerimiento/, a document that the Spanish colonizers read to the
indigenous peoples of Americas as they entered their land and
villages. The document certified the Spanish authority, in the name of
the king and the church, to take the land, its resources and people,
under Spanish control. It declared all who heard it subject to the
crown, and any those who resisted to be violently subdued. Mejias and
Couldry compare this document to the "Terms of Service" agreement from
Google Chrome, which also makes claims upon the user, in particular on
the "Content" that a user inputs into the system. The 2007 version of
the TOS asserts that,
#+begin_quote
You give Google a perpetual, irrevocable, worldwide, royalty-free, and
non-exclusive license to reproduce, adapt, modify, translate, publish,
publicly perform, publicly display and distribute any Content which
you [the user] submit, post or display on or through, the Services.
(Rpt. in Mejias & Couldry 12).
#+end_quote
More recently, the wording of TOS has muted the explictness of this
language. Recent wordings of Google TOS, the authors point out, don't
need to be as assertive: "without most of us ever having read those
original terms and conditions.... the extraction of data is already
well underway" (Mejias & Couldry 12). The more recent strategy has
shifted from assertive to excessive and at times obscure language
(these TOS now occupy many pages). For example, the 2024 Google TOS,
which is now held on its own subdomain, https://policies.google.com,
phrases like "operating and improving the services" and "to customize
our services for you" offer the necessary cover for "using automated
systems and algorithms to analyze your content" ("License", "Google
Privacy and Terms"). Additionally, the TOS contains strange and
difficult to parse formulations, like "We need your permission if your
intellectual property rights restrict our use of your content. You
provide Google with that permission through this license" ("License",
"Google Privacy and Terms"). The meaning appears to be that Google
will need, yet already has obtained, permission from the user. But
what exactly is one giving permission for Google to do? The TOS does
not say. 

That is if one even bothers to read the terms of service. Whereas the
/Requerimiento/ relied on blunt language claiming an authority over
land and people, more recent TOS rely on the user not even checking
the terms. While these approaches, separated by centuries, differ
dramatically, they both rely on the user not knowing the extent of the
rights being taken from them. The /Requerimiento/, similar to
modern-day TOS, used a language that was obscure to its audience. As
Mejias and Couldry point out, "[the conquerors] read this document in
Spanish... to indigenous or First Nation peoples who did not speak
Spanish!" (13). The legal strategy, then, maintains this dependence on
a certain level of ignorance from the user.

***** Shoshana Zuboff's "informate"
It is a cycle, as preciently pointed out by Shoshana Zuboff in 2015,
that thrives on data's status as what she calls an "electronic text."
Zuboff, whose 2015 essay ""Big Other: Surveillance Capitalism and the
Prospects of an Information Civilization" (and followup 2018 book,
/The Age of Surveillance Capitalism: The Fight for a Human Future at
the New Frontier of Power/) helped to formalize the notion of capture
by captial as the "logic of accumulation" (78).[fn:4] Her essay
analyzes papers written by Google’s Chief Economist Hal Varian, where
he discusses the potential of "computer-mediated economic
transactions" (Rpt. Zuboff 78). Varian identifies four new kinds of
business activities made possible by computation, which are "data
extraction and analysis," "new contractual forms," "personalization
and customization," and "continuous experiments" (Zuboff 78). Most of
these rely on the nearly infinite streams of data that platforms can
capture from users, and on the fact that these streams are mostly
hidden from view, thus creating asymmetrical relationships between the
consumer and the business, which obviates the role of trust. As Zuboff
explains, "New possibilities of subjugation are produced as this
innovative institutional logic thrives on unexpected and illegible
mechanisms of extraction and control that exile persons from their own
behavior" (85).

"Surveillance" capitalism is not as new as Zuboff would have it. She
describes it alternately as "A new economic order," "A rogue mutation
of capitalism," " a new instrumentarian power" (from book, /The Age of
Surveillance Capitalism/). As critics like Simone Brown and Ruha
Benjamin point out, however, the surveillance logic has existed for
centuries. Indeed, slavery, as an economic order, could be described
in the same terms that Zuboff uses to describe surveillance
capitalism: "A parasitic economic logic in which the production of
goods and services is subordinated to a new global architecture of
behavioral modification" (from book, /The Age of Surveillance
Capitalism/). Brown's work surfaces the quantifying and policing
practices that Black people have endured since slave ships. More
recently, Benjamin shows how new technologies perpetuate older
patterns under the guise of technical values like "neutrality." While
"tech advances are sold as morally superior," as Benjamin points out,
"they could not exist without data produced through histories of
exclusion and discrimination" (5). Values such as "objectivity,
efficiency, profitability, and progress" work to effectively siphon
the creator from these legacies perpetuated in the product (Benjamin
7). She explains that "tech designers encode judgments into technical
systems but claim that the racist results of their designs are
entirely exterior to the encoding process" (Benjamin 11-12).

While surveillance capitalism is certainly not new, Zuboff does point
out something else, an emergent effect of surveillance.

#+begin_quote
when it comes to information technology, automation simultaneously
generates information that provides a deeper level of transparency to
activities that had been either partially or completely opaque. It not
only imposes information (in the form of programmed instructions), but
it also produces information... This produces action linked to a
reﬂexive voice, as computer mediation symbolically renders events,
objects, and processes that become visible, knowable, and shareable in
a new way. (76)
#+end_quote

Zuboff uses the term "informate" to describe this tendency of computer
mediated action to create byproducts of information that were
previously inaccessible, even unimaginable. According to Zuboff, this
tendency turns all digital data "electronic text"--text which is ripe
for extraction via reading. When computers automate action, they
create new texts, like foam rising from a boiling pot.

Just Changed How AI Crawlers Scrape the Internet-at-Large;
Permission-Based Approach Makes Way for A New Business Model”)
#+end_quote

The argument refers to the original purpose of large-scale web
crawling, which was to populate and otherwise assist search engines.
This activity of taking content in the form of search results was
legitimized in a famous copyright lawsuit against Google, /The Authors
Guild Inc., et al. v. Google, Inc./ (2015). In this case, the judge
ruled that search results, such as that from Google Books, were
considered "fair use" because they point users to the original source
without diminishing its value. As long as search engines direct
traffic to the original source, then there appears to be no
infringement. 
 
Cloudflare points out that today's scrapers and crawlers to do not
direct users back to the original source; rather, the AI training
process /severs/ the data completely from its context, including its
source.[fn:3] Web pages are taken from their context and compiled into
massive collections which are fed, bit by bit, into a machine learning
algorithm. The severed connection to the source cannot be monetized,
"depriving content creators of revenue... and satisfaction."

As a solution, Cloudflare is shifting their policy to protect media
outlets and publishers. They have begun to offer the "permission based
model," where companies must pay content creators for access to their
web data. If the scraper does not have explicit permission to retrieve
data, then they will be blocked. The announcement of Cloudflare's new
policy came with written endorsement from publishers and media
outlets. Roger Lynch, CEO of media conglomerate Condé Nast, which owns
/Vogue/, /The New Yorker/, /Architectural Digest/, /Vanity Fair/,
/Pitchfork/, /Wired/, /Bon Appétit/, /Ars Technica/, among others,
declares that, "Cloudflare’s innovative approach to block AI crawlers
... is a critical step toward creating a fair value exchange on the
Internet that protects creators, supports quality journalism and holds
AI companies accountable" (“Cloudflare Just Changed How AI Crawlers
Scrape the Internet-at-Large; Permission-Based Approach Makes Way for
A New Business Model”). Of course, there is no garuntee (or even
expectation) that such payments would reach the pockets of actual
content creators, authors, copywriters, artists, who are populating
these sites with their work. If the new policy rewards someone, its
not the original creator but the publisher who enters into agreements
with the tech companies. In that sense, the "fair value exchange"
cited by Lynch is in fact another way of further monetizing what is
already an extractive practice of capture.

**** manipulating HTTP: Heritage Foundation
Websites are stored in web servers, accessed over a network. Web data
travels over the network in the form of bytes, the smallest portable
data unit. When the bytes reach the web browser, they are decoded into
HTML language, which is assembled into a web page readable by human
eyes.

Here is an example some the HTML from the homepage of /The Heritage
Foundation/, on ~https://Heritage.org~. This section of the page shows

/Web scraping/ is the extraction of such HTML data from websites. This
extraction is possible by manipulating HTTP, or HyperText Transfer
Protocol, which is a wrapper language that encapsulates the bytes that
travel over the network, like an envelope. This wrapper language
stipulates strict instructions on the transit of bytes from the web
server to the web browser. It not just the envelope, then, it is also
detailed protocol for how the envelope may be opened.

A web scraper uses programming code to manipulate an HTTP request.
Using HTTP protocol, it makes a request to a web server much like a
user makes a request by typing in a URL into the browser. The data it
gets back from the HTTP request is also the same as the data that
comes to a browser after searching a URL---only the data is
intercepted before it arrives at the browser, before it can be
assembled into a web page.

Below is an example of the code used to scrape the home page of /The
Heritage Foundation/ website. The code uses the ~bs4~ and ~requests~
libraries in python.[fn:17]

#+begin_src python
  from bs4 import BeautifulSoup
  import requests
  r = requests.get('https://www.heritage.org')
  source = r.content
  soup = BeautifulSoup(source, 'html.parser')
#+end_src

data from a server. The contents of request consist of a list of

**** Blockers and the resistance
Web servers, however, employ various forms of "blockers" or mechanisms
that deny the HTTP request. And as automated scrapers increase in
frequency, so do the bot blockers.[fn:18]

Over the past two years, Cloudflare, which runs on 20% of total
internet pages and 50% of pages based in the US, has added increasing
bot detection like "Challenges" and "Turnstiles" to its web pages.
Some of these operate under the surface and virtually invisible to the
user, such as checking the HTTP request metadata like the
"User-Agent." By default, the User-Agent is set to the name of web
browser and operating system making the request.

For example, my Firefox browser running Mac OSX has the User-Agent
~Mozilla/5.0 (Macintosh; Intel Mac OS X 10.15; rv:152.0)
Gecko/20100101 Firefox/152.0~. The python ~requests~ library, by
contrast, sets the User-Agent to ~python-requests/2.32.3~, making any
HTTP request using that library easy for blockers to detect.

Beyond examining HTTP request data, bot blockers will use JavaScript
code to check if the request is from a real web browser, rather than
from an automated program. JavaScript, which is a programming language
native to web browsers (and discussed in more detail in Chapter 4:
Text Display), can inject challenges for the browser to solve,
challenges which may or may not involve the user. All of these
challenges might require some processing from the browser, and
increasingly, they may require the user's interaction (to confirm that
the request comes from a real browser, not a bot). One popular
challenge is the "security verification" checkbox, which is
increasingly visible across the internet (See Fig #).

[[./images/verify.png]]

While these kinds of blockers are more advanced, there are ways of
overcoming them. One effective method involves using a virtual or
"ghost" browser through python libraries like ~selenium~. Instead of
sending an HTTP request via a python script, ~selenium~ routes the
request through an actual web browser---though one that is operated
remotely by the python script. When the program is running, a browser
window will open, seemingly on its own, navigate to a web page, and
click on links---all without the input of the user's mouse. Which is
why they are called "ghost browsers". One major drawback to this
method is that it requires a virtual display, which many "headless"
scraping servers do not have, in order to access the data via the
browser window. That requirement, in addition to the increase in
necessary processing power, detracts large-scale web crawling.
