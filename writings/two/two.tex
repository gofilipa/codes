\subsection{Doubt}
\label{sec:org37f0ad2}
1 The novel \emph{Orlando: A Biography} (1928), by Virginia Woolf, famously
opens with an assertive gender designation followed by an immediate
qualification: “He—-for there could be no doubt of his sex, though the
fashion of the time did something to disguise it" (11).

2 In order to perform quantitative text analysis on this text, the
standard tasks of “pre-processing” would evacuate this sentence of its
gender qualification. Text analysis, a process which involves
calculating and visualizing textual patterns, requires transforming
the source text into a computable format. Pre-processing the text
(also called “cleaning” or “regularizing” the text) would strip
capitalized words, punctuation, “stop words” (such as articles and
prepositions), and inflections in word endings, all of which are
deemed to be semantically minor, and wouldn't affect the analysis of
more substantial features like nouns, verbs, adverbs, and adjectives.

3 Cleaning the first sentence not only would strip it of its pronouns,
including the gender assertion in the first word, “He.” It would also
cut the em dash immediately following this “He,” signaling the
entrance of the narrator and his conspicuous certitude: “—for there
could be no doubt of his sex….” Only the following list of words would
remain:

\begin{quote}
‘could’, ‘doubt’, ‘sex’, ‘though’, ‘fashion’, ‘time’, ‘something’,
‘disguise’
\end{quote}

4 Text analysis requires removing such "minor" elements of language in
order to find connections among more "substantial" terms. None of
these features, however, are minor in a text about a fictional
biography of a 16th-century English nobleman who undergoes a sex
change. Orlando's gender, as many critics point out, develops along
with his story.\footnote{Criticism of the novel about gender and story.} His trials and tribulations experienced as a man
are resolved when he becomes a woman, and she experiences a form of
self-actualization that not only transcends time but also, for a
moment, stops it. If Orlando has a thesis about gender, it is
this--gender is what one makes of it. It relies fantasy, imagination,
and most importantly, language, in order to come into being.

5 This chapter examines how quantitative text analysis works to
analyze gender by using Woolf’s \emph{Orlando} as a test case. It explores
an experimental approach to text analysis that deconstructs gender
binaries by drawing connections between computer programming and
gender theory--Judith Butler's theory of gender performativity. This
analysis surfaces a mechanism common to both text analysis and gender
performativity, which is \emph{iteration}. There is an iterative method
that works on both text analysis and on gender which, I argue, can be
harnessed to resignify text analysis procedures. This chapter proposes
a text analysis procedure that iterates through various gender terms
in \emph{Orlando}, to re-signify their meaning within binary constraints.
The goal here, as with Butler's own work, is to work within
constrictive structures, like the gender binary, in order to unmake
them from the inside.

\subsection{Falsifiable}
\label{sec:orgc963cb3}
1

Text analysis has always been driven by great ambitions. Matthew
Jockers's book, \emph{Macroanalysis: Digital Methods and Literary History}
(2013), "ask[s] very big questions\ldots{} regarding how literary trends
are employed over time, across periods, within regions"; Ted
Underwood's book, \emph{Distant Horizons: Digital Evidence and Literary
Change} (2020) traces the "larger arcs of literary change that have
remained hidden from us by their sheer scale." Most recently, with the
advent of text generation methods, Leif Weatherby's book, \emph{Language
Machines: Cultural AI and the End of Remainder Humanism} explains that
language models which "'pretrain' on massive corpuses of text
data—trillions of words scraped mostly from the internet", bring
language into "the age of its algorithmic reproducibility".

Predating (and perhaps inspiring) much of this work in text analaysis
is an even greater ambition by from a literary scholar named Franco
Moretti. Morretti, who introduced many both within and without
Literary Studies to text analysis, published a polemical essays on
"distant reading" in a collection of the same name in 2008. In 2017,
his research caught the attention of the \emph{New York Times}, who
published a profile detailing his "radical cry" for a new kind of
literary analysis. Distant reading, according to Moretti, involves
counting up textual features to derive textual patterns and make
inferences. It is a kind of analysis where "distance" as Moretti
explains, "is a condition of knowledge" (48).

2

accused Moretti of sexual crimes. The accusations ranged from
unsolicited touching to rape. One of his accusers was a first-year
graduate student in English.
\begin{quote}
When I was a graduate student at UC Berkeley in 1984-85, my
then-professor Franco Moretti sexually stalked, pressured, and raped
me. He specifically said to me, 'You American girls say no when you
mean yes.'[\ldots{}] I reported him to the Title IX officer, who was then
Frances Ferguson, Ph.D. She was a friend of his and urged me not to
make a report[\ldots{}] I told Franco about my conversation with Ferguson,
and he threatened to ruin my career if I pressed charges against him.
He said he had powerful friends who were attorneys who would ruin my

I have told a number of people about it privately, however. These are
upstanding, well-known professors of history and English at other
institutions, who would certainly corroborate my story. (Kimberly
Latta "Op-Ed: What Happened?" 2017).
\end{quote}
When the women came forward, Stanford went no further than claiming
they would review the cases. There has been no record of an inquiry,
investigation, or any repercussions for Moretti (Liu, Knowles,
"Harassment, assault allegations against Moretti span three
campuses"). He remains an Emeritus Professor at the same institution.

3

Moretti's writing has an easy, conversational flow. He uses short
sentences, but with varied syntactic structures and frequent commas,
giving his prose a sense of rhythm. He also weaves in references
across fields of knowledge as disparate as evolutionary theory,
systems theory, and Marxism. Despite this intellecutal baggage, his
arguments move at a light clip, and seem to circle methodically around
a drain so that, as long as you're following him, there appears to be
no alternative. The effect is entracing.

But there is something suspect about his confidence. Not just in his
ambition, which is shared by several others, but the way he offers his
arguments. He transforms inferences, premises, and hypotheses into
declarative statements. Paragraphs begin with sentences like,
\begin{quote}
So, the market selects the canon. But how? (69).

Yes, forms \emph{can} move in several directions. But \emph{do} they? (emphasis
original, 112).

So. As novelistic forms travel through the literary system, their
plots are (largely) preserved, while their styles are (partly)
lost---and replaced by 'local' ones (134).

What did the birth of a consumer society mean for the European novel?
More novels, less attention (175).

The major metamorphosis of eighteenth-century titles is simple: in the
space of two generations, they become much, much shorter (182).

As the market expands, titles contract; as they do that, they learn to
compress meaning; and as they do \emph{that}, they develop special
'signals' to place books in the right market niche (204).
\end{quote}
These quotes, taken from mostly different essays in \emph{Distant Reading},
show Moretti's characteristic way of stating a claim or hypothesis as
a conclusion, as if the point is already self-evident. To be fair,
many of these points do synthesize arguments and evidence from
previous paragraphs. They are, some of them, more summary of previous
points than completely new ones. But it is the way they are presented
here that strikes me. Their apparent solidity, the lack of a
subjective "I" to ground them, the presumptuousness and ease with
which they make room for themselves in the world. It is not
confidence, it is something more than confidence, which felt exciting
at the same time that it felt dangerously familiar.

4

One of Moretti's earliest essays, "The Soul and the Harpy" sows the
seeds that would eventually grow into distant reading. Published in
1983, two years before the date of the first report of assault of
against him, this essay lays out new methodological standards for
literary criticism. In it, he appears to be very concerned about the
apparently unstable methodology of close reading, which drives
contemporary literary criticism; he betrays a deep suspicion about
this and other analytical practices that “multiply, rather than
reduce, the obstacles every social science encounters when it tries to
give itself a testable foundation” ("Soul" 22). He likens the literary
critic to "Narcissus", "whose only pleasure lay[s] in contemplating
his own reflection (“Soul” 14).

Moretti proposes an alternative methodology, a “falsifiable
criticism.” This method grounds the critical process in hypotheses to
“test” literary interpretations, pursuing answers that are “coherent,
univocal, and complete” (“Soul” 21). But a close look at his reasoning
suggests that the goal may not be so much to verify interpretations,
but to falsify them.
\begin{quote}
“The day criticism gives up the battle cry ‘it is possible to
interpret this element in the following way,’ to replace it with the
much more prosaic ‘the following interpretation is impossible for such
and such a reason,’ it will have taken a huge step forward on the road
of methodological solidity” (“Soul” 22).
\end{quote}
To squash the possibility of alternative interpretations. For literary
criticism to achieve a means of attaining irrefutable conclusions.
Moretti here implicitly aligns literary criticism with methodologies
from the sciences, known as reproducible methods.\footnote{Sarah Cohen-Boulakia, Khalid Belhajjame, Olivier Collin, Jérôme
Chopard, Christine Froidevaux, Alban Gaignard, Konrad Hinsen, Pierre
Larmande, Yvan Le Bras, Frédéric Lemoine, Fabien Mareuil, Hervé
Ménager, Christophe Pradal, Christophe Blanchet, Scientific workflows
for computational reproducibility in the life sciences: Status,
challenges and opportunities, Future Generation Computer Systems,
Volume 75, 2017, Pages 284-298, ISSN 0167-739X
\url{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.future.2017.01.012}.} In those
disciplines, reproducibility means that the analytical procedure will
yield the same result.\footnote{Reproducibility and transparency: what's going on and how can
we help. Nat Commun. 2025 Jan 27;16(1):1082. doi:
10.1038/s41467-024-54614-2. PMID: 39870612; PMCID: PMC11772749.}

5

Moretti's nascent idea of falsifiable criticism finds a methodology in
quantification, that is, methodologies based on the counting of
textual features. Quantitatification, according to Moretti, simplifies
text into workable units. Language in all its complexity is flattened
into features which can be counted and then graphed. As he explains,
“fewer elements, hence a sharper sense of their overall
interconnection” (\emph{Graphs, Maps, Trees} 1). These fewer elements, he
argues, enable ambitious interpreptations: "If we want to understand
the system in its entirety, we must accept losing something. We always
pay a price for theoretical knowledge" (\emph{Distant Reading} 49). What is
lost, textual detail and specificity, is a worthwhile price for the
potential reward. "The more ambitious the project," Moretti explains,
"the greater must the distance be" (\emph{Distant Reading} 48).

6

In one famous essay from \emph{Distant Reading}, "Style, Inc.," Moretti
reduces entire novels to just their titles. Here, he explores what
titles, which get shorter and shorter over the 18th century, might
reveal about trends in the book market. Moretti describes his
analytical process:
\begin{quote}
[F]irst, I describe a major metamorphosis of eighteenth-century
titles, and try to explain its causes; next, I suggest how a new type
of title that emerged around 1800 may have changed what readers
expected of novels; and finally, I make a little attempt at
quantitative stylistics, examining some strategies by which titles
point to specific genres. Three sections, three pieces in the large
puzzle of the literary field. (\emph{Distant Reading} 181-2; emphasis mine)
\end{quote}
Moretti's critical method involves posing hypotheses, assembling and
analyzing data, making inferences, and occasionally, reframing the
original hypotheses. Yet, he repeatedly understates his interpretive
moves at each stage of his analysis: “try to explain,” “suggest,”
“make a little attempt.” The diminutive language here not only belies
the presumptions his arguments make, for example, that titles function
as a proxy for market forces, "a code, in the market: half sign, half
ad\ldots{} where the novel as language meets the novel as community" (181).
It also misrepresents the role that inferential thinking plays in the
conclusions he draws:
\begin{quote}
As the market expands, titles contract; as they do that, they learn to
compress meaning; and as they do \emph{that}, they develop special
'signals' to place books in the right market niche. (204)
\end{quote}
As Stephen Ramsay points out, Moretti presents his own insights as a
description of reality, as if "data is presented to us\ldots{} not as
something that is also in need of interpretation" (Ramsay, \emph{Reading
Machines} 5). Moretti here performs an agential sleight-of-hand:
diminishing his own interpretive role while amplifying the agency of
inanimate phenomena like book titles. Titles may fluctuate, and as
they do so, his analysis deftly shows, patterns emerge around the
consolidation of nouns and adjectives. But they do not, on their own,
\emph{learn to compress meaning} or \emph{develop special 'signals'}.

7 

Distant reading deliberately overlooks details which, as some have
aptly pointed out, are actually necessary to cultural critique.
Details of text would express what has been traditionally minimized or
marginalized. In an MLA panel titled "Distant Reading After Moretti,"
Lauren Klein explains,
\begin{quote}
[I]t’s not a coincidence that distant reading does not deal well with
gender, or with sexuality, or with race. Gender and sexuality and race
are precisely the sorts of concepts that have been exposed and
interrogated by attending to non-dominant subject positions. And like
literary world systems, or "the great unread," the problems associated
with these concepts, like sexism or racism, are also problems of
scale, but they require an increased attention to, rather than a
passing over, of the subject positions that are too easily (if at
times unwittingly) occluded when taking a distant view.
\end{quote}
Klein's own work tracing these elements, for example, in hidden
networks within archival documents, proves how careful attention to
what is minor or oblique can surface previously suppressed
stories.\footnote{Klein, Lauren F., "The Image of Absence: Archival Silence, Data
Visualization, and James Hemings," in \emph{American Literature}, 85 (4):
661–688, 2013.} In one project, Klein uses network analysis methods to
bring forth the "ghost" of James Hemmings, an emancipated slave who
figured in the correspondence of a famous slaveholder. By marking up
oblique references in the letters of his former master (Thomas
Jefferson), Klein "render[s] visible the archival silences implicit in
our understanding of chattel slavery today" (665). Her work
demonstrates that the details of Hemmings's life, which is deeply
distributed in the correspondence data, requires a kind of reading
that is close and careful.

8

In opposition to close and careful reading, the ambition to grasp
large swaths of literary history may be a proxy for avoiding certain
kinds of cultural critique. After all, Moretti says "we know how to
read texts, now let's learn how \emph{not} to read them" (\emph{Distant Reading}
48). The proxy functions through an amplification and valorization of
what is seemingly objective--for example, the counting of words. With
word counts and their resulting graphs and charts doing the work of
interpretation, one might finally \emph{close down} close reading and the
pesky critiques asssociated with it. One might finally approach a
\emph{falsifiable criticism}, to be able to say once and for all that "the
following interpretation is impossible for such and such a reason"
(Moretti, “Soul” 22).

9

This faith in the falsifiable--in the power of counting words--infects
even those who disavow quantitative methods. In a detraction written
by Nan Z. Da, which rallied the Computational Literary Studies debate
when it was released in 2020,\footnote{Expand on the computational literary studies field and debate.

At the time of its release, Da's article stoked many responses from
literary scholars involved in text analysis. See Bode, "What’s the
Matter with Computational Literary Studies?"} Da vehemently opposes the use of
quantification in critical interpretation. Da argues that quantitative
methods, in fact, reveal a "fundamental mismatch between the
statistical tools that are used and the objects to which they are
applied"--that is, to literary objects (620, 601). She emphasizes her
point by attempting to reproduce an experiment in "topic modeling," a
machine learning method that generates a number of "topics," or
keywords, from large collections of text. When she attempts to
replicate a topic modeling experiment on her own computer, she
produces different results.

10

Despite her oppositional stance toward quantitative tools, Da has
something in common with those who champion them. She fundamentally
\emph{agrees} about the objectivity of their procedures. As Ben Schmidt
writes about Da's stance, “Far more than anyone I’ve seen in any
humanities article, she asserts that scientists do something arcane,
powerful, and true" (Schmidt, "A computational critique of a
computational critique of computational critique."). Despite their
vastly different views on the role of quantitative methods for
studying literature, Da and Moretti appear to agree that these methods
ought to provide results that are, at the very least, reproducible.

\begin{itemize}
\item Katherine Bode's critique: “what seems a complete divergence of
opinion obscures a fundamental agreement: that computation is
separate from literary phenomena” ("What's wrong with Computational
Literary Studies")
\end{itemize}

11

There are those who disagree about the apparent objectivity of
reproducible methods. Ted Underwood, for example, explains that
"algorithms are actually bad at being objective and rather good at
absorbing human perspectives implicit in the evidence used to train
them" (“Machine Learning and Human Perspective” 92). Underwood creates
what he calls "perspectival models" of literary data, where models
represent different "perspectives." In one project, on studying gender
in novels, Underwood uses a binary classification algorithm to cluster
terms that male and female writers tend to associate with either
gender. From the results, he creates a chart which visualizes the
differences between male and female writers and their treatment of
gender. In this chart (see Fig. 1), there are two diagonals. The
diagonal moving from the bottom-left to top-right corner represents
where the genders agree on gendered terms. The bottom-left shows
agreement on terms used to describe the male gender ("pocket",
"grinned"), and the top-right shows agreement on the female gender
("eyes," "mother"). The opposite diagonal, running from the top-left
to bottom-right corners, shows disagreement. The top-left shows terms
that each gender tends to use to describe their own gender
("wondered", "mind") and the bottom-right shows terms that each gender
uses to describe the opposite gender ("meant", "kissed"). The chart,
according to Underwood, shows two \emph{perspectives} on gender--the male
and the female. Rather than making claims to gender in an objective
sense, it dramatizes a perspectival shift between the two genders.

12

Unfortunately, Underwood's methodology, as he himself admits,
reinscribes the gender binary. This happens not only in the choice of
model, a binary classification model which makes predictions on a
yes/no or, in this case, a male/femal scale (predicting whether a term
will be more associated with a male or a female gender). It also
happens with the choice in visualizing the results in a way that
places male and female genders in opposition. The diagonal going from
the bottom-left to the top-right, which finds similarity between the
genders, reinforces a stereotypical active/passive binary (among other
binaries) between men and women. Similarly, the diagonal going from
the top-left to the bottom-right, although based on agreement between
the genders and their relationships to themselves, nonetheless imposes
an \emph{opposition} between how male and female authors describe each
other. The graph effectively instantiates its implicit assumption that
male and female writers will oppose the other by visualizing this as a
feature.

Not only does the diagram reify the gender binary, it effectively uses
the gender binary \emph{as a model} for proving the value of quantitative
methods. This is an effective reversal of how data and models are
supposed to work in support of each other. When explaining the
diagram, Underwood says that "gender theorists will be frustrated by
the binary structure of the diagram\ldots{} I needed a simple picture,
frankly, in order to explain how a quantitative model can be said to
represent a perspective" (“Machine Learning and Human Perspective”
98). However, the "perspective" which he represents is one where the
two genders are already diametrically opposed. The gender binary is a
necessary presumption in order for the experiment to take place--a
prior binary that then makes the choice of a binary-structured model
appear appropriate. For his diagram to work, gender roles must have
been already manufactured into opposition. In other words, binary
gender is the model through which his argument about perspectival
modeling can make sense.

Add Bode's critique.

Bode’s position is that QLS
ought to begin by considering the types of gaps and biases in
evidence. She wants critics to incorporate analyses from textual
studies prior to running statistical tests. We should build QLS out of
these areas of humanistic inquiry that center interpretation. There
are certain processes in statistical analysis that maintain bias and
unknown assumptions: sampling and comparison do not get an accurate
picture if what we are searching for is unfindable or harder to find. 

Responding to Ted Underwood’s Distant Horizons. Critique on
“perspectival modeling” as ignoring ethical issues that persist with
scale.
\begin{itemize}
\item “Underwood’s proposal to replace data modeling with sampling and

\end{itemize}

13

There are valid and even provocative reasons to study gender as a
binary system. But the questions that drive such experiments must be
differently derived. Questions like "how to distinguish one gender
from another gender" effectively reify existing structures of the
binary. Alternatively, other work uses text analysis to \emph{deconstruct}
social categories. In their introduction to The Journal for \emph{Cultural
Analytics}'s “Identity Issue,” Susan Brown and Laura Mandell explain
that, “The goal is to acknowledge the subjective effects of belonging
to an identity constituted historically through oppression without
believing that the identity itself exists independently from
historical conditions” (6). 

14

For example, Laura Mandell studies gender through genre. Mandell
deploys the popular stylometry measurement, “Burrow’s Delta,” which
visualizes the “distance” between writing styles by creating branches
("deltas") between different texts. She finds that Mary
Wollstonecraft's style is shared across comparable male writers like
William Godwin and Samuel Johnson: “Wollstonecraft’s sentimental
anti-Jacobin novels most resemble Godwin’s sentimental anti-Jacobin
novels… whereas her essays most resemble Johnson’s writings” ("Gender
and Cultural Analytics:: Finding or Making Stereotypes?" par. 29). Her
analysis creates new categories that draw gender into genre--squaring
the gender binary. She creates doubly refracted categories like, "men
writing as men," "women writing as women," "women writing as men,"
"men writing as women" (par. 35).

15

When using reproducible tools, it bears asking what is being
reproduced. Applying text analysis to racial categories, Edwin Roland
and Richard Jean So explore whether a machine can correctly identify
the race of a novel's author. The point, they are careful to say, is
not to test whether the machine is correct, but to examine how the
categories themselves are constructed. As they explain, "the machine
simply measures the robustness of the social constructedness of these
given categories and points out what gives them such vigor" ("Race and
Distant Reading" 69). Analyzing a large corpus of novels by white and
black authors, So and Roland find that black authors generally display
more varied vocabulary than white authors. From this result, they
infer that White authorship, as a category, only coheres \emph{as a
category} against the variance of Black authorship. In other words,
White authorship is only visible as a category when it is compared to
Black authorship, which displays overall more varied uses of language
forms.

Interestingly, this quantitative exercise points Roland and So toward
a peculiarity in the results: that the algorithm wrongly categorizes
Black-authored novel, James Baldwin's \emph{Giovanni’s Room} (1956), as
being written by a White author. This misclassification is due to a
single word--"appalled"--whose usage, according to the model, has
generally been a signal of White authorship. The term only occurs once
in the text, in an early scene where the narrator David describes his
strained relationship to his father: “I did not want to be his buddy.
I wanted to be his son. What passed between us as masculine candor
exhausted and appalled me” (Rpt. in Roland, So 71; emphasis mine).
Given the connotation of whiteness in "appalled" and its middle French
root, “apalir” (to grow pale), Roland and So explain that this
term--which emerged as a result of the computer's mistake--enables
their analysis to coordinate gender to race: “the moment David
develops a troubled relationship to normative masculinity [as] also
the moment he becomes ‘White’” (71). Rather than reify racial
categories, this computational experiment points out that they are
constructed on flimsy terms.

16

So and Roland began with the binary form, the binary categories of
Black/White, but they ended with its fracturing. This fracturing,
evident in the computer's misclassification of authorship in
\emph{Giovanni's Room}, leads to a reading which coordinates race with
gender and sexuality. This "queering the machine’s color line," the
authors explain, opens up new readings of intersectionality in the
novel which "in their current form, our data and model are not robust
enough to handle" (72).

Rather than reproducible, this method is refractive, relfecting (and
corrupting) the constructed nature of binary structures back at the
critic. This method, as I explain in the next section, takes an
\emph{iterative} stance toward gender forms in literary data.

\subsection{Iteration in gender}
\label{sec:org3f40dee}
1

Multiple text analysis projects draw from Butler's concept of
performativity. In particular, they take the language of performance
and qualities of the performative to describe and conceptualize their
methodologies. For example, Mandell's project, introduced above, takes
the behavioral quality of gender performativity and associates it with
authorial style, asserting that both gender and genre "are\ldots{} highly
imitatable," so that "anyone can adopt gendered modes of behavior,
just as anyone can write in genres stereotypically labeled M/F" (par.
30). Bode applies the "performative" to illustrate the analytical act
itself, which necessarily intervenes and becomes inseparable from data
as object of analysis, where "peformativity" is "attuned to the
coconstitution of computational methods and objects" ("What's the
Matter with Computational Literary Studies"). Her project, \emph{Reading at
the Interface}, explores how "meaning is not carried by texts but
produced in situated interactions between readers, texts and
contexts," including computational tools.\footnote{Bode, Katherine. “Data beyond representation: From computational
modelling to performative materiality.” MLA Convention, Seattle, 10
January 2020.
\url{https://katherinebode.wordpress.com/home/mla-convention-2020/}} Another project, from
the field of Computational Humanities, Kent K. Chang, Anna Ho and
David Bamman cite Butler's performativity as motivation to
computationally "measure subversion" in TV show dialogue. Their uses a
language model to predict whether samples of a TV show, \emph{The Big Bang
Theory}, are queer-coded. If the model marks certain sections of
dialogue between hetersexual characters as queer, they argue, then it
will have indicated moments of "subversion" in the show (Chang et
al.).

2

Unlike those projects, however, this project draws from
performativity's methodology in depth. In doing so, it demystifies a
common misreading of performativity--that it denotes an act or series
of acts that can be imitated at will. Indeed Butler's follow up to
\emph{Gender Trouble: Feminism and the Subversion of Identity} (1990),
aimed to clarify how performativity is bound by social systems and
norms in ways that delimit agency. In this work, \emph{Bodies that Matter:
on the Discursive Limits of Sex} (1996), Butler explains that gender
performativity is a compulsory process that constitutes the subject.
In Butler's reasoning, the body only materializes (\emph{matters}) through
the repetition of gender norms. And it is through repetition that the
body also assumes subjectivity. Crucially, "there is no subject who
decides on its gender\ldots{}. on the contrary, gender is part of what
decides the subject" (ix). In other words, gender is an activity that
determines both the body and subjectivity. It is from this context of
a compulsory gender system where the subject must find a way to
re-work the terms of expression.

3

Butler's argument for performativity draws from poststructuralist
feminist theorist Luce Irigaray. Irigaray is best known for her
readings of phallogocentrism (the idea that the phallus is a focus or
lense for understanding other phenomena), in the works of of classical
and modern philosophers, like Plato, Aristotle, Descartes, and Kant.
Irigaray finds that these philosophers represent the feminine through
masculine terms, "on the basis of masculine parameters," as she
describes in her book, \emph{The Sex Which Is Not One} (Irigaray 23). Her
conceptualization of the feminine's circumscription within the gender
binary directly influences Butler's conceptualization of
performativity and how it might resist rigid significatory systems
(18).

4

Irigaray's analysis of the feminine centers on its unrepresentable
status within dominant framings like the gender binary. She argues
that the binary of male/female effectively \emph{precludes} the
representation of the feminine: "The economy that claims to include
the feminine as the subordinate term in a binary opposition of
masculine/feminine excludes the feminine, produces the feminine as
that which must be excluded for that economy to operate" (Butler 10).
While the feminine is effectively excluded from the system, it
conditions the representation of terms \emph{within} the system. This
excluded feminine, this "constitutive outside," makes possible the
creation of the inner feminine, the "domesticated" feminine. It makes
legible an internal feminine. She, what Butler calls the "unspeakable"
feminine, is the \emph{matter} from which the binary may come into
intelligibility.


5

This external feminine is \emph{matter}: the generative, creative material
from which distinctions emerge. Butler explains that,

\begin{quote}
every explicit distinction takes place in an inscriptional space that
the distinction itself cannot accomodate. Matter as a \emph{site} of
inscription cannot be explicitly thematized. And this inscriptional
site or space is, for Irigaray, a \emph{materiality} that is not the same
category of "matter" whose articulation it conditions and enables\ldots{}
this second inarticulate "matter" designates the constitutive outside
of the Platonic economy; it is what must be excluded for that economy
to posture as internally coherent" (emphasis original, 12-13).
\end{quote}

Irigaray's association of this excessive feminine with "matter" draws
simultaneously from a classical analogy between the form/matter and
masculine/feminine, in the Aristotelian tradition, and from the
etymological association between the feminine as \emph{mater}, mother, and
significantly, as \emph{matrix}, the generative material for all life. It
is this \emph{matrix}, this "inscriptional space", which cannot be
articulated, but from which all distinctions of male/female can
emerge.

6

How is this feminine to enter into representability? How can it be
invoked directly without subscribing itself to the ruling structure?
Or, in Butler's formulation, “how can one read a text for what does
not appear within its own terms?” (\emph{Bodies} 11). As a constitutive
exclusion which makes the intelligible binary possible, the excluded
feminine cannot be represented in the same terms (10). 

7

The answer, as it turns out, invovles breaking open significatory
procedures. Butler considers the referential power of language: “Can
language simply refer to materiality, or is language also the very
condition under which materiality may be said to appear?” (6). For
example: the physical body. In order to refer to a physical body,
language must first assume a body. That is, the body must be posed \emph{in
language} first. Therefore, Butler reasons, “signification produces as
an effect of its own procedure the very body that it nevertheless and
simultaneously claims to discover as that which precedes its own
action” (6). In their characteristicly clausey prose, Butler explains
that signification \emph{creates} the body which it appears to reference.
Which leads them to a major realization: "the mimetic or
representational status of language\ldots{} is not mimetic at all. On the
contrary, it is productive, constitutive, one might even argue
performative' (\emph{Bodies} 6). 

8

Performativity, then, denotes the perpetual creation and recreation of
norms, a discursively-based, significatory cycle which \emph{produces} that
which it appears to reference. As they explain, "performativity must
be understood not as a singular or deliberate 'act,' but, rather, as
the reiterative and citational practice by which discourse produces
the effects that it names" (xii). According to Butler, it is through
this discursive performativity that "sexed" bodies emerge: "'Sex' is
not a simple fact or static condition of a body, but a process whereby
regulatory norms materialize 'sex' and achieve this materialization
through a forcible reiteration of those norms" (xii). What appears as
physical or biological category, "sex," is effectively delimited by
and through a discursive framing. Bodily materiality is a
"sedimentation" formed through repetition, whereby meaning is
signified and re-signified endlessly, and never completely.

9

Categories like sex and gender, as Butler explains, are never stable,
but require constant repetition:

\begin{quote}
This "being a man" and this "being a woman" are internally unstable
affairs. They are always beset by ambivalence precisely because there
is a cost in every identification, the loss of some other set of
identifications, the forcible approximation of a norm one never
chooses, a norm that chooses us. (86)
\end{quote}

Repetition stabilizes gender identiy against a field of potential
exclusions, what Butler calls the "cost in every identification."
Every identification creates this field of exclusion. And it is this
material which will come back to destabilize coherence. With an
ominous overtone, Butler explains that, "The political terms that are
meant to establish a sure or coherent identity are troubled by this
failure of discursive performativity to finally and full establish the
identity to which it refers" (Butler 140).

10

This necessity to repeat gender signification is a weakness, which
Butler will use as an exploit. For, within this significatory circle,
lies the possibility of \emph{resignifying} terms. Resignification happens
by repeating what has been excluded from the significatory economy,
“citing” what Butler calls a “repudiated” meaning. As Butler explains,
every "identification takes place through a repudiation which produces
a domain of abjection (xiii). This domain of abjection, containing
identities that are repudiated, offers "a field of disruptive
possibillities" for resignification (Butler 10). Butler explains,
"this disavowed abjection will threaten to expose the self-grounding
presumptions of the sexed subject" (xiii). Through this threat, "what
has been foreclosed or banished from the proper domain of "sex"\ldots{}
might at once be produced as a troubling return" (Butler xxx).

11

If the troubling return is repeated continually, Butler argues, the
stability of the original identity will be brought into question.
Butler offers an example with the term "queer," which is a term that
harnesses its own history of repudiation for political purposes,
"resignifying the abjection of homosexuality into defiance and
legitimacy (Butler xxviii).

\begin{quote}
The compulsion to repeat an injury is not necessarily the
compulsion to repeat the injury in the same way or to stay
fully within the traumatic orbit of that injury. The force of
repetition in language may be the paradoxical condition by
which a certain agency---not linked to a fiction of the ego
as master of circumstance---is derived from the impossibility
of choice. (183)
\end{quote}

It is through the force of repetition that repudiated meanings, which
necessarily draw from insulting and painful pasts, come to alchemize
new significations. The paradox is that what appears to be a cyclical
or even robotic behavior takes on an agentic capacity. Automata, at
some point, attain agency.

12

Subversion thus reaches toward a destabilization of the ruling system.
And this is where Butler's theory is often misunderstood. For example,
Chang et. al's text analysis project applies the label of "subversive"
to queer-coded characters within an otherwise heterosexual TV show,
claiming that: "gender performativity provide[s] the essential
framework for understanding how characters on TV subvert traditional
gender roles and expectations" (4). Their analysis finds that two of
its two of the main characters from \emph{The Big Bang Theory}, Raj and
Howard, share dialogue that is romantic and intimate in nature. The
language model tags many instances of their dialogue as dialogue
between partners. And indeed, these characters display an atypical
intimacy which is the target of many of the show's jokes. The authors
argue that "subversion," for this project, emerges in "an undercurrent
of emotional intimacy and dependency that challenges the rigid
boundaries of what is considered acceptable" (Chang et al., 10). In
their conclusion, they posit that, "Raj and Howard offer more than
occasional punchlines; they represent an instance of queer love: they
have to invent a form of togetherness for themselves" (Chang et al.,
2024, p. 9). However, according to Butler, subversion is more than
exhibiting a non-dominant norm. It is doing so in a way that
challenges established norms. Butler carefully stipulates against
instances of apparent subversion which effectively prop up
heterosexual regimes:

\begin{quote}
[T]here are forms of drag that heterosexual culture produces for
itself—-we might think of Julie Andrews in \emph{Victor, Victoria} or
Dustin Hoffmann in \emph{Tootsie} or Jack Lemmon in \emph{Some Like It Hot}
where the anxiety over a possible homosexual consequence is both
produced and deflected within the narrative trajectory of the films.
These are films which produce and contain the homosexual excess of any
given drag performance, the fear that an apparently heterosexual
contact might be made before the discovery of a nonapparent
homosexuality. This is drag as high het entertainment, and though
these films are surely important to read as cultural texts in which
homophobia and homosexual panic are negotiated, I would be reticent to
call them subversive. (85)
\end{quote}

Despite being queer-coded, these characters remain within hetersexual
relationships. As a result, their queernesss effectively "produce[s]
and contain[s] the homosexual excess" within the parameters of the
show. They use humor to temper these excesses. Borrowing from
Irigaray, they are an example of "domesticated" queerness, rather than
subversive queerness. 

13

Subversion always aims toward destabilization. In doing so, repetition
is key, enabling the introduction of the repudiation back into the
system. Returning to Irigaray’s writing, Butler explains that Irigaray
achieves subversion by "mim[ing] philosophy\ldots{} and, in the mime,
tak[ing] on a language that effectively cannot belong to her" (12).
Butler cites a passage where Irigaray \emph{mimes}, or repeats, the
language of Plato. Her repetition, however, has a particular effect:
she "performs a repetition and displacement of the phallic economy"
(Butler 18). In an interesting double-level inhabitation, Butler
approximates what Irigaray must be feeling to inhabit Plato's written
style:

\begin{quote}
I will not be a poor copy in your system, but I will resemble you
nevertheless by miming the textual passages through which you
construct your system and showing that what cannot enter it is already
inside it (as its necessary outside), and I will mime and repeat the
gestures of your operation until this emergence of the outside within
the system calls into question its systematic closure and its
pretension to be self-grounding.” (\emph{Bodies} 18)
\end{quote}

Irigaray undermines the authority of the masculine through repetition,
by “cit[ing] Plato again and again, but the citations expose precisely
what is excluded from them, and seek to show and to reintroduce the
excluded into the system itself” (Butler 18). Butler explains that
"This is a taking of his [Plato's] place, not to assume it, but to
show that it is \emph{occupiable}\ldots{} a kind of overreading which mimes and
exposes the speculative excesses in Plato (emphasis original, 11).

14

Through repetition, Irigaray displaces the logic of phallogocentrism,
introducing something external to the system while simultaneously
remaining within its terminology. Deception emerges from resemblance,
and insubordination through subservience. The key is repetition, or
more precisely, what Butler refers to as \emph{iteration}, a continual
miming of the authorizing norm. Through the iterative process, one
brings back again and again the repudiated meaning as a troubling
return. "The task," according to Bulter, "will be to consider this
threat and disruption\ldots{} as a critical resource in the struggle to
rearticulate the very terms of symbolic legitimacy and
intelligibility" (xiii).

\subsection{Iteration in python}
\label{sec:org052eebb}

1

Iteration is a core concept in programming. Iteration means repeating
the same action to each bit of data in a collection of data. It drive
one of the most important programming structures, called the "loop,"
which \emph{iterates} through collections of data like text, performing the
same operation to each word. Iterating or "looping through" words in a
text will count their features. And counting, as I explain below, is
the foundation of all text analysis tasks, from word clouds to text
generation.

4

Before counting text, a text must first be standardized or "cleaned."
The looping mechanism quickly cleans a text and remove extraneous or
undesirable elements analysis. Elements that are highly frequent, like
articles, prepositions, and pronouns, can drown out other terms which
are considered more substantial, like nouns and verbs. Additionally,
irregular cases also need to be standardized, because capital letters
would count toward different words (for example, "talk" and "Talk"
would be considered two different words). For similar reasons, word
endings and inflections like plural forms (endings in "s") and tenses
('walked', 'walks' or 'walking') are converted into a root form. Once
the text has been regularized, then the counts will be more accurate
to its content.

Using a loop, a person may \emph{iterate} through each word in a text,
check whether it needs to be "cleaned," and then perform a set of
cleaning actions. For example, a first round of cleaning might check
for the presence of punctuation and capital letters. Below is an
example of a \texttt{for loop} that that filters out punctuation and
transforming any capital letters into lowercase forms:

\begin{verbatim}
more time to run.
  clean = [] 
  for word in text:
      if word.isalpha():
          clean.append(word.lower())   
\end{verbatim}

3

The loop begins by creating an empty placeholder, \texttt{clean}, where words
will be saved in their clean form. after passing the filters below.
The next line begins the for loop, which iterates through each word in
the text, in our case, in Woolf's novel, \emph{Orlando}. The third line
contains the first filter, \texttt{isalpha()}, which checks if the word is
comprised of only alphabetic characters (i.e. it is not a number or
punctuation). If the word is alphabetic, it passes the filter. The
word moves to the next line, where it is lowercased, \texttt{lower()} and
finally added to \texttt{clean}. When the loop is done running over every
word in the text of \emph{Orlando}, the final list of words in \texttt{clean} will
only contain alphabetic and lowercased letters.

5

The next task in cleaning is to remove what are called \texttt{stops} or
"stop words,” which consist of prepositions, articles, pronouns, and
auxiliary verbs.

The loop takes each word the \texttt{clean} list, and checks to see if that
word is also contained in above list of \texttt{stops}. If the word is not a
stop word, then it will be added to a new list, \texttt{no-stops}. After this
processing, the following words remain from the first sentence of
Orlando:

\begin{verbatim}

 'disguise']

As mentioned in this chapter's introduction, the gender pronouns, "He"
and "his", as well as the em dash following "he" has been removed.

The final step of cleaning involves stripping word inflections to get
the word's root form, or "lemma". This process involves looking up
each word, one by one, to return the shortest lemma found. It uses the
WordNet database, an electronic lexical database used for
computational linguistics. WordNet contains every word's root form, as
well as "synsets" or synonym sets, representing underlying
relationships between words.\footnote{Christiane Fellbaum (1998, ed.) WordNet: An Electronic Lexical
Database. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.} In the python code, the lemmatizer
function \texttt{lemmatize()} is called from the \texttt{WordNetLemmatizer} module.

\begin{verbatim}
lemmatized = []
for word in no-stops:
    lemma = WordNetLemmatizer.lemmatize(word)
    lemmatized.append(lemma)    
\end{verbatim}

The above code iterates through the list of \texttt{no-stops} (which has
already been stripped of punctuation, numbers, capitalization, and
stop words) and lemmatizes each individual word using the
\texttt{lemmatize()} function. It saves the word to a new variable, called
\texttt{lemma}, which it finally appends to the list called \texttt{lemmatized}.
After iterating through every word in the no-stops list, and
lemmatizing each one by one, the text is ready for analysis.

Text analysis is, at a most basic level, about counting words. From
counting how frequently a word appears, one can start to derive
patterns about word usage. 

In python, text analysis tasks are based on a module called \texttt{Counter},
which contains the functions for starting and keeping track of word
counts. The logic, which is simplified below from the source code,
(written originally in the C programming language\footnote{link to source Counter (python) code:
\url{https://github.com/python/cpython/blob/main/Lib/collections/\_\_init\_\_.py};
link to source code in C language:
\url{https://github.com/python/cpython/blob/main/Modules/\_collectionsmodule.c}}) keeps track
of how many times each word has appeared while going through a text
one word at a time.

\begin{verbatim}
counts = {}

for word in text:

    if word not in counts:
        counts[word] = 0

    counts[word] = counts[word] + 1
\end{verbatim}

For every word, the program checks whether it has already been seen
before, starting with the statement \texttt{if word not in counts}. If it has
not been seen, the program runs the indented line below to set the
count to zero. Then, moving to the following line, the count is
increased by one to represent the current appearance. However, if the
word \emph{has} already been seen, the program will skip the 0 line and
instead retrieve the existing count, to increase it by one again. By
repeating this process for every word, the program builds a complete
record of how many times each unique word occurs.

The counts for the first sentence of the text are the following:

\begin{verbatim}

       'doubt': 27,
       'sex': 53,
       'though': 94,
       'fashion': 11,
       'time': 154,
       'something': 73,
       'disguise': 4,
       ...})
\end{verbatim}

The logic of this counting loop is fully articulated in another
programming language, the C language. The C language is a lower-level
language that underlies Python code. In that language, the
instructions for the same loop appear with the following syntax.

\begin{verbatim}

while ((key = PyIter_Next(it)) != NULL) {

  oldval = PyDict_GetItem(mapping, key);

  if (oldval == NULL) {
      newval = 1;
  }
  else {
      newval = oldval + 1;
  }

  PyDict_SetItem(mapping, key, newval);
}
\end{verbatim}

The C loop, which begins with \texttt{while}, updates a value by one: \texttt{newval
= oldval + 1}. While the code in the C language is more convoluted
than Python, it runs more efficiently. This is because C code can run
at lower levels of the software stack, which is where computation and
counting actually occurs. Python, by contrast, needs to be translated
("compiled") in order to be understood by lower-level machine
processes.

For that purpose, another function, called \texttt{similar()} displays words
that are used in similar contexts of the target word. This function
takes a canonical concept from NLP called "distributional similarity,"
where words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar
meanings. For woman, those words are the following,

To compute the results of \texttt{similar()}, the program first takes the
context of the target word from \texttt{concordance()}. Then, it counts each
of the words surrounding woman. As seen above with the \texttt{Counter()}, it
keeps a running tally of these words. Then, it searches other words in
the database and, again, takes a count of surrounding words for these
words. It then looks for words that have the most shared contexts,
that is, the most shared words within their context window, with the
word woman. Finally, it ouputs a list of terms that share the most
contexts with the word.

For the program then, word meaning is computed through patterns from
its usage based on word counts. This concept is a foundational one in
all text analysis going into deep learning. Importantly, the computer
does not understand or impute \emph{meaning} to the words. Rather, it only
counts each word as a string, that is, as a piece of data composed of
alphanumeric sequences. From there, it derives meaning from patterns
of usage.


13

That is, in a nutshell, what so-called \emph{deep learning} methods do.
Deep learning methods, like those that drive generative text processes
(discussed in detail in chapter 5) move from displaying the results of
word counts into generating word predictions.

Many of these methods are based on the concept of "word embeddings" or
"word vectors" to ascribe machine-interpretable meaning to strings.
Like \texttt{similar()} and \texttt{concordance()}, word embeddings work from
patterns of word similarity based on context. Unlike the previous
methods, however, word embeddings encode numeric values to a given
word based on its context. They are called "vectors" because these
numeric values represent points in a graph. By graphing word
similarity, word vectors translate relationships of word context into
geometric space.

14

a list of numbers that represent a high similarity score between
“woman” and another word.

Based off a popular model trained from twitter posts, the vector which

a similarity score, or “weight,” of .93, or 93\%, then with “mother,”
with .92, then “father,” with .90, and so on.

Commonly, word vectors are organized into a matrix, or tabular,
format. Using this matrix format, further mathematical operations are
possible using statistics, linear algebra, and calculus—-the building
blocks of deep learning methods.

The inventors of word vectors introduced their power to the world via
a formula that uses math to compute language meaning (Mikolov et al.
2013). The famous formula uses a mathematical equation to calculate
the meaning of the word "Queen". Here, the vector for the word "queen"
is calculated by subtracting the vectors of "man" from "king," and
finally, adding the vector for "woman."

\begin{quote}
vector("Queen") = vector("King") - vector("Man") + vector("Woman")
\end{quote}

The paper, whose insights about technology of word vectors made Large
Language Model development possible, began with this formula. It's
cross-cultural intelligibility, therefore, cannot be understated.
Gender, which is necessarily imbricated with imperialist concepts,
becomes an operand for computing language meaning. Like Ted
Underwood's graph which puts gender into an oppositional relationship,
gender is the \emph{model} which shows the algorithm to be true.

The word vector for "Queen consists of numbers, a long list of
numbers.

\begin{quote}
'queen': -0.3583, -0.6767, 0.1689, -0.6164, -0.0135, -0.2284, 0.3359,
        0.1149, -0.1512, -0.8014
\end{quote}

Within the long list of probabilities, each individual probability
score represents that word's contextual relationship to another word
in the dataset. One of these numbers may represent Queen's
relationship to the word "Woman," and this number will have a higher
probability score than that which represents the relation to the word
"man." These long chains of numbers, which encode these probability
scores between "Queen" every other individual word in the database,
function effectively like the word’s definition. In kind of
literalization of the poststructuralist floating signifier, a single
word is defined as, not what it means in itself, but how it relates to
(and differs from) every single other word. In word vectors, then,
meaning emerges from association, from proximity and distance, between
words.

Like Butler's matter or \emph{matrix} which produces the binary of
masculine/feminine, here is another matrix that produces meaning
according to masculinist parameters. The matrix format of the word
vector enables a host of mathematical operations and algorithms: from
example, cosine similarity calculates the distance between two
vectors, that is, two words; and regression analysis guesses the
relationship between an independant and dependant variable, or
relation between one word and another.

16

In the following section, I use these word vectors as a starting point
to explore terms related to gender terms in Orlando, starting with the
terms “woman” and “man.” Although I start with the gender binary, word
vectors enable me to quickly proliferate beyond it. Rather than
resolving gender into an oppositional structure or a neat formula, it
cracks open.

\begin{quote}

6 Here, “delicious” describes a refusal, then a yielding—-the
vacillations of what appears to be a passive form of pleasure,
ostensibly opposed to the active pleasure of pursuit which Orlando

feminine experience of pleasure, about withholding and, eventually,
submitting to the active force, it is a pleasure rooted in what is not
quite passivity and not quite power.

7 To get a deeper understanding of this term, I run another similarity

8 “Culpable,” from the Latin “culpa,” meaning fault, denotes a body
deserving of blame for its refusal to conform. Here, the word modifies
“laggardry,” which describes the obstinacy or persistency of Orlando’s
love for women, despite that she is now a woman herself. It seems
that, for Orlando, desire is defined by a sense of guilt about
refusing to conform. The next usage of this term occurs soon after,
when Orlando reprises her earlier thoughts about the deliciousness of
refusal:

\begin{quote}
‘To refuse and to yield,’ she murmured, ‘how delightful; to pursue and
conquer, how august; to perceive and to reason, how sublime.’ Not one
of these words so coupled together seemed to her wrong; nevertheless,
as the chalky cliffs loomed nearer, she felt culpable; dishonoured;
unchaste, which, for one who had never given the matter a thought, was
strange. (120)
\end{quote}

9 Rehearsing the conventional roles of the sexes, roles which Orlando
fails to fit into, she feels (in addition to “culpable)
“dishonour[ed]” and “unchaste”–-words associated with an ideal of
virtuous femininity. This feminizing language deepens the relationship
between desire, guilt, and deliciousness, the last of which was
previously characterized as pleasure in a passivity that is not quite
powerless. Here, Orlando’s refusal to conform to social expectations,
for which she feels culpable, constitutes another kind of passivity, a
form of refusal. The connection between “delicious” and “culpable”
seems to define queer desire as a distinctly passive mode of
resistance.

11 Here, the narrator’s evocative language undermines his pretense to
objectivity. This slip into figurative language eventually grows into
a crisis of signification that recurs persistently through the novel.
This crisis will eventually affect not only language’s ability to
signify, but also that of gender. As Victoria L. Smith affirms, “what
happens in the novel… and what it thematizes—language’s inability to
adequately represent the ‘thing itself’—mirrors the undecidability of
the text—is it a biography, an autobiography, fantasy, etc.—and the
impossibility of the form of ‘woman’” (58).

\end{quote}

13 Orlando, deep in depression, struggles to understand the role of
figuration in language. Much like the narrator who is suspicious of
figurative language, Orlando also questions the truthfulness of
figuration. First, he attempts plain language, “the sky is blue,” “the
grass is green,” but these prove insufficient for describing a sky
that moves “like the veils which a thousand Madonnas have let fall
from their hair” and grass that “fleets and darkens like a flight of
girls fleeing the embraces of hairy satyrs from enchanted woods.”
Orlando, who has just been abandoned by a woman, sees flight and
modesty, qualities which he finds “false,” in nature. The word
“despaired” here, like “chequered” above, appears in a moment that
elevates the role of figuration in language. It also marks a crossing
of the crisis of signification over the diegetic threshold, into
Orlando’s internal world. These terms, which occur in scenes where the
lines between fiction and reality, and internal and external narration
are blurred, elevate the importance of language’s imaginative
capacities in the novel.

coordinated to gender. I examine the co-occurrence of words from both
the “woman” and “man” lists within a single passage, the scene where

\begin{quote}

15 The passage constellates the crisis of signification within the
larger issue gender ambiguity. Orlando uses seemingly arbitrary
metaphors, “A melon, an emerald, a fox in the snow,” indicating that
at the same time which he cannot place Sasha’s gender, he also cannot
find the right words to describe her. As Sasha’s gender oscillates
between male and female throughout the passage, so the narrative voice
alternates between Orlando’s interiority and the narrator’s
commentary. Taking this crisis of signification beyond the diegetic
narrative, the narrator’s “pause” signals the constructed nature of
the scene. This constructed quality is emphasized by the narrator’s
explanation for Orlando’s choice of words, which have no “reason,” and
“were mostly taken from things he had liked the taste of as a boy.”
Language, like gender, thus becomes a tool for representation and
performance.
