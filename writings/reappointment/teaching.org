This teaching statement covers my teaching philosophy, the courses
I've taught at Pratt, curriculum development, mentoring and advising,
and future plans.

** Philosophy
As a language scholar who uses digital tools, I have experience
teaching in two general areas: English and computer science. In the
area of English, I have taught courses on academic writing, critical
thinking, and Latinx literature. On the side of computer science, I
have taught courses Python programming, natural language processing,
data science, and machine learning.

From this background, I have developed a teaching philosophy that
blends together humanist and computational skills, taking a humanist
approach to computational tools and objects. This philosophy is
organized into three core principles:

#+begin_quote
1. the close is critical;
2. writing as an active learning method; and
3. knowledge is always contingent
#+end_quote

In what follows, I explain these core principles in more detail.
*** 1) the close is critical:
In my courses, regardless of their format or focus, I teach close
reading as an essential critical practice. Originating from literary
studies, close reading is a particular kind of qualitative analysis
that sustains attention on a very small selection of evidence,
traditionally, a passage in a text. I find that close reading helps
students not only to analyze evidence, like textual passages, depth,
but also to generate new ways of using and interpreting all kinds of
data.

For example, in my INFO 664: Programming Cultural Heritage course, I
have students consider large datasets on an intimate scale,
demonstrating how attention to detail can reveal new material for
analysis and critique. In my INFO 664 class from last fall, I had
several students develop projects which used Small Language Models
(SLMs) to examine gender bias in popular culture,[fn:1] to study
discourses of toxic online spaces,[fn:2] and to surface patterns in
sensationalist language[fn:3]---three projects which ended up winning
the 2026 Info Show's ``People's Choice Award.''

*** 2) writing as an active learning method:
I am a strong proponent of writing as a powerful and irreplaceable
skill for professional work. All of my courses emphasize the
development of this skill---all the more forcefully when emerging AI
technologies threaten to reduce students' intellectual capacities to
the capacityof any given tool.

Writing is, in my experience, the most efficient method of active
learning: it requires externalizing one's inner understanding in a
coherent and material way, while also exposing it to alternative
perspectives and real-world issues. In my courses, I teach writing
both through low stakes, daily formats like freewriting exercises, and
in long term assignments like formal papers. For example, in my INFO
656: Machine Learning course, the major assignment is a conference
paper that students complete in groups and submit to a machine
learning (ML) conference. This assignment not only keeps them up to
date with recent advances in a rapidly changing field, it also
encourages students to think deeply about their contributions to ML
research within a professionalizing context.

*** 3) knowledge is always contingent:
One of the first lessons I learned in the classroom is the importance
meeting students where they are, regardless of their previous
training. One way of doing so is to invite students to use their lived
experience as a ground for understanding and critique---to assure them
that their own histories, combined with reflection, constitute a
valuable kind of knowledge. A necessary corollary to knowledge as
experience is to accept that there can be no single perspective for
any question or issue. Rather than a single perspective, there are
degrees of visibility made available through habit and privilege.

In my teaching, I try to expand what is visible through the exposure
to a wide range of theoretical points of view, particularly in the
interdisciplinary selection of readings from my courses like INFO 601,
INFO 656, and INFO 657. Readings in these courses explore fields such
as Black Feminist Studies, Archival Studies, and AI Ethics research,
which address similar topics through different initial questions,
assumptions, approaches, and conclusions.

** Curriculum Development
In Summer 2025, I taught a new course, INFO 697: How to Build a Bot,
which combines hands-on programming with critical examination of
coding, automation, and the social implications of bots. I plan to
continue developing the curriculum through additional courses in
intermediate-level programming. As I explain in more detail in the
Future Plans section below, I plan to develop a Python course focused
specifically on working with text-based data, building on a workshop I
currently offer through the Data Storytelling Lab.

Throughout the past academic year, I have also worked on revising the
DH Certificate to bring it up to date with the most recent theories
and tools in DH. In collaboration with my colleague, John Decker, we
re-organized the DH Certificate into two knowledge areas or
``buckets,'' (1) Theories and Methods and (2) Technologies, to reflect
changing trends in DH scholarship as well as to better professionalize
students for careers in DH and related work.

** Mentoring & Advising
In addition to the 30 or so students that I teach directly each
semester, I currently advise 35 students across all four programs at
Pratt SI. Each semester, I meet individually with these students to
discuss their progress in coursework and projects, to strategize their
final portfolios, and career expectations and goals.

I also advise two students groups: the /Stacks/ Student Journal and
Latines in Tech. The /Stacks/ Student Journal is a new group founded
by students from my INFO 601: Foundations of Information course. These
students are in the process of producing their first issue of the
/Stacks/ journal, which will contain peer-reviewed versions of their
601 papers, showcasing the high level of critical work and writing
accomplished in that course. The second group, Latines in Tech, is
professional development group primarily focused on Latinx students.
Their main goals are to provide professional development and
networking opportunities for their members. Currently, I am working
with that group to organize a community altar for to celebrate Dia de
los Muertos at the end of October.

** Future Plans
My future plans involve developing the following:
- In collaboration with my colleague Kathy Carbone, I plan to develop
  a study abroad program based in Lisbon, Portugal. The program will
  likely focus on an intersection of DH and archival work, though we
  are still in the early stages of discussing the possibilities.
- I also plan to develop a ``Readings in Queer Studies'' seminar style
  course. While I am aware that theoretically heavy courses have been
  less popular at Pratt SI, I think students' appetite for theoretical
  material is growing. In courses like INFO 601: Foundations of
  Information and INFO 657: Digital Humanities, the assigned readings
  in queer studies generate much interest and discussion, especially
  among students in LIS, who often claim them as their favorite
  readings. For this course, I would complement the seminar focus with
  fieldwork activity that involves visiting queer archives in New York
  City.
- Finally, I plan to develop a Python programming course that is
  specific to working with text-based data. This course would build
  from my workshop that I run for the Data Storytelling Lab, which
  focuses on using advanced tools for text cleaning, processing,
  annotating, and NLP analysis. This course would join my other
  course, INFO 697: How to Build a Bot, to offer instruction in
  intermediate programming skills.
