This statement covers my research agenda, recent papers, and future
plans since beginning my position at the Pratt Institute School of
Information in Fall 2024.

\section*{Research Agenda}
\label{sec:org94fdd85}
I am a language scholar that uses digital tools to study language
related to gender, sex, and sexuality. While my formal training is in
English literature, with focuses in queer studies and the digital
humanities (DH), I work at the intersection of the humanities and
computer science (CS) disciplines, and aim my research toward
audiences in AI ethics, computational linguistics, queer studies,
trans studies, and DH-related fields.

To that end, I pursue a research agenda based on three main goals:
\begin{quote}
\begin{enumerate}
\item to interrogate the contradictions of present-day theorizations of
gender, sex, and sexuality;

\item to read computational mechanisms and their effect on language
meaning; and

\item to communicate to audiences across humanities and CS disciplines.
\end{enumerate}
\end{quote}

From Fall 2024 to Fall 2026, I have written five papers, two that have
been published in peer-reviewed venues, and three that are currently
being considered for publication at peer-reviewed venues. In addition,
I am also completing a manuscript for an academic book, which is due
in spring 2027.

In what follows, I explain the details of my research work and how
they fit into the goals described above.

\subsection*{Goal \#1: to interrogate the contradictions of present-day theorizations of gender, sex, and sexuality}
\label{sec:org948c4e5}
Over the past decade, the discourse around gender, sex, and sexuality
has been highly polarized around positions like the ``anti-gender
movement,'' ``gender-critical feminism'' and ``Trans Exclusionary
Radical Feminism'' (TERFism), which have moved into the
mainstream.\footnote{Bassi, Serena; LaFleur, Greta (2022). "Introduction: TERFs,
Gender-Critical Movements, and Postfascist Feminisms". Transgender
Studies Quarterly. 9 (3): 311–333. \url{https://doi.org/10.1215/23289252-9836008}} These largely reactionary movements are driven by
resistance against so-called ``gender ideology'' and, in response,
seek to ground gender in ``the biological reality of sex.''\footnote{The White House. “Defending Women From Gender Ideology
Extremism And Restoring Biological Truth To The Federal Government.”
The White House, January 21, 2025.}

I approach these contemporary discourses on gender, sex, and sexuality
as an information scholar interested in how categories and
representations are encoded and operationalized in datasets,
computational systems, media, and other information infrastructures.
Taking these contemporary discourses on gender, sex, and sexuality as
a starting point, my research interrogates and disentangles some of
the contradictions that constitute social and cultural understanding
of these terms through computational analysis. This work aims not only
to parse out ideological investments and contradictions among these
terms, but also to surface areas of potential overlap and unexpected
consensus between polarized views.

In two recent papers, both of which are currently being considered at
peer-reviewed venues, I explore how trans studies theorizing of gender
as an expressive, material, and behavioral phenomenon opens a
potential site of alignment between ideological perspectives on
gender. The first paper, ``What LLMs Can't See About Gender,'' under
review at \emph{FAccT 2027}, discusses the topic of gender bias in Large
Language Model (LLM) evaluation methods. This method draws from recent
theorizing in trans studies to show how current methods in language
modeling limit the ability to detect, or \emph{see}, gender as an identity
that operates visibly in the world. The second paper, ``'My Body Feels
Like it’s Coming Off': on Cis Embodiment in \emph{Love Is Blind},'' under
review at \emph{TSQ: Trans Studies Quarterly}, reads experiences of bodily
dissonance in a reality TV dating show that features cisgender and
heterosexual participants. The paper argues that, despite their
dramatically different self-identifications and life experiences,
trans and cis-hetero subjects have similar experiences in how they
relate to their own bodies and, in some cases, how they desire
normativity.

\subsection*{Goal \#2: to read computational mechanisms and their effect on language meaning}
\label{sec:org00cdd51}

This research goal is main goal of my book, \emph{Queer Code: Re-working
Language Technologies}, which is under contract with Bloomsbury
Academic Press. The book explores how language technologies, like web
scraping, text analysis, and generative AI, reduce language complexity
to make it manageable and computable at large scales. These
increasingly automated processes encode dominant logics and extractive
incentives onto language data, creating well-documented issues with
data sovereignty, privacy, and most recently, social bias and
discrimination, as current AI tools demonstrate.

The book demonstrates how to re-work technological processes that
constrain or reduce language meaning by looking to queer studies
concepts that resist oppressive structures from within. Drawing from
concepts like Judith Butler's Gender Performativity and José Esteban
Muñoz's theory of Dis-Identification, for example, the book re-works
constraining computational mechanisms into new analytical
methodologies that open up novel readings of language data.

\subsection*{Goal \#3: communicating to audiences across humanities and CS disciplines}
\label{sec:orgb71141a}
My research aims to engage knowledges that otherwise may not come into
contact with one another. Over the past two years, I have written
three papers for fields like computational linguistics, AI ethics,
trans studies, and digital scholarly editing, which span humanities
and CS disciplines.

My CS papers include ``Some Myths About Bias: A Queer Studies Reading
Of Gender Bias In NLP,'' published in the \emph{Association for
Computational Linguistics Proceedings} in August 2025, ``Small
Language Models and the Synthetic Middle,'' currently under review at
\emph{FAccT 2027}, and the aforementioned ``What LLMs Can't See About
Gender,'' also under review at \emph{FAccT 2027}. These papers draw from
theorizing in queer studies and trans studies in order to make
recommendations to CS fields. ``Some Myths About Bias'' argues that
queer studies theorizing on the binary reveals certain traps of
"binary thinking" that drive bias detection and mitigation techniques
in Natural Language Processing (NLP). The other paper, ``Small
Language Models and the Synthetic Middle,'' incorporate the humanistic
method of close reading into a computational experiment to study
linguistic overlaps (the ``synthetic middle'') between polarized
discourses on gender and transgender rights in the USA.

On the side of the humanities, my papers include ``Re-encoding
Dominance: Queer Approaches To TEI Markup,'' published in \emph{Digital
Editing \& Publishing in the 21st Century} in April 2025. This paper
examines how a language technology, TEI (Text Encoding Initiative), a
markup standard for encoding text-based cultural heritage materials
into preservable formats, alongside theories from queer studies. Much
like my work on the \emph{Queer Code} book, the goal of this paper is to
read technical mechanism as a heuristic for how power operates in
social and cultural norms---in this case, that data formats
disambiguate data into modular components in ways that delimit what
said.

\section*{Future Plans}
\label{sec:orgafad9c3}
My most immediate plan is to continue my work on the \emph{Queer Code}
book, which is due (in manuscript) on March 31, 2027.

I also intend to explore the issue of ideological polarization around
gender in a more sustained way, perhaps in a subsequent book devoted
primiarily to that topic. Moving beyond my work in \emph{Queer Code}, which
draws in part from my doctoral disseration in literary studies, I see
myself focusing on language in contemporary and popular discourses,
such as popular journalism and reality TV, as my most recent work
demonstrates. Given my background and training, I feel an urgency to
apply literary and queer studies frameworks toward analyzing
ideologically-encoded discourses that proliferate increasingly across
information networks.

Finally, it is important to my values as a scholar and educator that I
continue to make this research legible across disciplines and fields.
In light of that, I will continue to publish in CS and humanities
venues, rather than committing to one or the other, with the goal of
publishing one peer-reviewed article per year in each of these areas.
