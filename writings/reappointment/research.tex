This statement outlines my research agenda, recent papers, and future
plans since beginning my position at the Pratt Institute School of
Information in Fall 2024.

\section*{Research Agenda}
\label{sec:org47f9443}
I am a language scholar that uses digital tools to study language
related to gender, sex, and sexuality. While my formal training is in
English literature, with focuses in queer studies and the digital
humanities (DH), I work at the intersection of the humanities and
computer science (CS) disciplines, and aim my research toward
audiences in AI ethics, computational linguistics, queer studies,
trans studies, and DH-related fields.

To that end, I pursue a research agenda based on three main goals:
\begin{quote}
\begin{enumerate}
\item to interrogate the contradictions of present-day theorizations of
gender, sex, and sexuality;

\item to read computational mechanisms and their effect on language
meaning; and

\item to communicate to audiences across humanities and CS disciplines.
\end{enumerate}
\end{quote}

From Fall 2024 to Fall 2026, I have written five papers, two published
in peer-reviewed venues, and three currently under peer review. In
addition, I am also completing a manuscript for an academic book,
which is expected to be published in late 2027 or early 2028. 

In what follows, I explain the details of my research work and how
they fit into the goals described above.

\subsection*{Goal \#1: to interrogate the contradictions of present-day theorizations of gender, sex, and sexuality}
\label{sec:org910adcd}
Over the past decade, the discourse around gender, sex, and sexuality
has been highly polarized around positions like the ``anti-gender
movement,'' ``gender-critical feminism'' and ``Trans Exclusionary
Radical Feminism'' (TERFism), which have moved into the
mainstream.\footnote{Bassi, Serena; LaFleur, Greta (2022). "Introduction: TERFs,
Gender-Critical Movements, and Postfascist Feminisms". Transgender
Studies Quarterly. 9 (3): 311–333. \url{https://doi.org/10.1215/23289252-9836008}} These largely reactionary movements are driven by
resistance against ``gender ideology'' and, in response, seek to
define gender in the ``the biological reality of sex.''\footnote{The White House. “Defending Women From Gender Ideology
Extremism And Restoring Biological Truth To The Federal Government.”
The White House, January 21, 2025.}

I approach these contemporary discourses on gender, sex, and sexuality
from an information studies perspective---specifically, with interest
in how categories and representations, which are encoded into language
forms, can thus be decoded through computational methods. Taking these
contemporary discourses on gender, sex, and sexuality as a starting
point, my research interrogates and disentangles some of the
contradictions that constitute social and cultural understanding of
these terms through computational analysis.

In two recent papers, both of which are currently being considered at
peer-reviewed venues, I explore how trans studies theorizing of gender
as an expressive, material, and behavioral phenomenon opens a
potential site of alignment between ideological perspectives on
gender. The first paper, ``What LLMs Can't See About Gender,''
currently under review for a CS conference, discusses the topic of
gender bias in Large Language Model (LLM) evaluation methods. This
method draws from recent theorizing in trans studies to show how
current methods in language modeling limit the ability to detect, or
\emph{see}, gender as an identity that operates visibly in the world. The
second paper, ``'My Body Feels Like it’s Coming Off': on Cis
Embodiment in \emph{Love Is Blind},'' currently under review at a trans
studies journal, reads experiences of bodily dissonance in a reality
TV dating show that features cisgender and heterosexual participants.
The paper argues that, despite their dramatically different
self-identifications and life experiences, trans and cis-hetero
subjects have similar experiences in how they relate to their own
bodies---a similarity that opens a potential common ground between
initially opposed perspectives.

\subsection*{Goal \#2: to read computational mechanisms and their effect on language meaning}
\label{sec:org766a7a4}

My book, \emph{Queer Code: Re-working Language Technologies}, which is
under contract with Bloomsbury Academic Press, explores how language
technologies, like web scraping and generative AI, reduce language
complexity to make it manageable and computable at large scales. These
increasingly automated processes encode dominant logics and extractive
incentives onto language data, resulting in technologies that
perpetuate pre-determined and/or majoritarian meanings into language
forms, resulting in well-documented issues such as social
discrimination, as current AI tools demonstrate.

The book demonstrates how to re-work technological processes that
constrain or reduce language meaning by looking to queer studies
concepts that resist oppressive structures from within. Drawing from
concepts like Judith Butler's Gender Performativity, for example, the
book re-works constraining computational mechanisms into new
analytical methodologies that open up novel readings of language data.

\subsection*{Goal \#3: communicating to audiences across humanities and CS disciplines}
\label{sec:org2d70bbc}
My research aims to engage knowledges that otherwise may not come into
contact with one another. Over the past two years, I have written
papers for fields like computational linguistics, AI ethics, trans
studies, and digital scholarly editing, which span humanities and CS
disciplines.

My CS papers include ``Some Myths About Bias: A Queer Studies Reading
Of Gender Bias In NLP,'' published in the \emph{Association for
Computational Linguistics Proceedings} in August 2025, as well as
``Small Language Models and the Synthetic Middle,'' currently under
review at a CS conference, and the aforementioned ``What LLMs Can't
See About Gender,'' also under review at a CS conference. These papers
draw from theorizing in queer studies and trans studies in order to
make recommendations to CS fields. ``Some Myths About Bias'' argues
that queer studies theorizing on the binary reveals certain traps of
``binary thinking'' that drive bias detection and mitigation
techniques in Natural Language Processing (NLP). The other paper,
``Small Language Models and the Synthetic Middle,'' incorporates the
humanistic method of close reading into a computational experiment to
study linguistic overlaps (the ``synthetic middle'') between polarized
discourses on transgender rights in the USA.

On the side of the humanities, my papers include ``Re-encoding
Dominance: Queer Approaches To TEI Markup,'' published in \emph{Digital
Editing \& Publishing in the 21st Century} in April 2025. This paper
examines a language technology, TEI (Text Encoding Initiative), a
markup standard for encoding text-based cultural heritage materials
into preservable formats, alongside theories from queer studies. Much
like my work in the \emph{Queer Code} book, the goal of this paper is to
read how language meaning is determined by technical mechanisms---in
this case, that data formats disambiguate data into modular components
(TEI ``tags'') in ways that delimit what can be said.

\section*{Future Plans}
\label{sec:org08f4cf5}
My most immediate plan is to continue my work on the \emph{Queer Code}
book, which is due in manuscript on March 31, 2027.

While the thinking and argumentation in this book was birthed in a
literary studies context (drawing in part from my doctoral
dissertation), it has also led me more firmly into the analysis of
contemporary and popular discourses, such as popular journalism and
reality TV. My interest in this new area, which begun with the goal of
parsing out ideological investments and contradictions among gender
terms, has revealed unexpected sites of overlap and consensus, which I
want to pursue in a more sustained way.

After \emph{Queer Code}, then, I plan to explore the issue of ideological
polarization around gender in a subsequent book devoted primarily to
that topic. I would apply literary and queer studies training toward
analyzing these ideologically-encoded discourses that only seem to
intensify as their content proliferates in information networks.

In the meantime, however, it is also important to my values as a
scholar that I continue to make this research legible across
disciplines and fields. In light of that, I will continue to publish
in CS and humanities venues, rather than committing to one or the
other, with the goal of publishing one peer-reviewed article in each
of these areas per year. In doing so, I will continue to make the
results of my research legible to scholars across these disciplines.
