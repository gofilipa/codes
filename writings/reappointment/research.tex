\section{introduction}
\label{sec:org32bff22}
My work uses digital tools and methodologies to study language,
especially language related to gender, sex, and sexulaity. While my
formal training is in English Literature, with subfocuses in Queer
Studies and the Digital Humanities (DH), my work is at the
intersection of the Humanities and Computer Science disciplines,
including fields like Critical AI Studies and Computational
Linguistics.

Since beginning my position at the Pratt Institute School of
Information in Fall 2024, I pursue a research agenda based on three
main goals:

(1) to explore and critique computational mechanisms as heuristics for
cultural phenomena;

(2) to interrogate the contradictions and investments in present day,
social understandings of gender, sex, and sexuality; and

(3) to communicate across the disciplines.

Toward that end, I have written five papers, two of which have been
published in peer-reviewed venues, and three which are currently being
considered for publication at peer-reviewed venues. In addition, I
have also secured an academic book contract, with the complete
manuscript due in spring 2027.

In what follows, I explain the details of this work and how they fit
into the goals described above. 

\subsection{interrogating present day contradictions in gender, sex, and sexuality}
\label{sec:org8beb461}
My research takes contemporary discourses on gender, sex, and
sexuality as its primary subject. The aim of my research is to
disentangle some of the contradictions that constitute these
definitions accoring to the current debates, with the goal of finding
middle ground bewteen polarized views.

As of the past several years, the discourse on gender in particular is
highly polarized, with various waves of reactionary gender movements,
including "gender-critical feminism", "TERFism", among others. These
initiatives are largely driven by conservative arguments opposing
"gender ideology" or "gender theory," and grounding gender in
"biological" sex.

My work examines the apparent poles of this discourse to open a space
of connection, a middle ground between both sides. The idea is not to
resolve or settle the disagreements between them, but to demonstrate
that there are spaces of solidarity even been highly contrasting
views. It suggests the idea that gender ought to be defined as an
expressive phenomenon, which counters some more progressive views of
gender while supporting Trans Studies theorizing on the subject.

The first paper, "What LLMs Can't See About Gender," currently under
review at \emph{FAccT 2027}, examines Natural Language Processsing (NLP)
methods for for detecting and evaluating gender bias in language. The
paper argues that these methods, which use Large Language Models
(LLMs) to detect and evaluate gender bias, tend to define gender as an
internal phenomenon, as interior, "personal" feelings of
identification, which is associated with a progressive view of gender
identity.\footnote{Jack Drescher. 2025. \href{https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/gender-dysphoria/what-is-gender-dysphoria}{What is Gender Dysphoria?} \emph{American
Psychiatric Association}.} This paper critiques this practice, demonstrating that
it limits what LLM bias detection and evaluation methods are able to
perceive. Rather, it recommends developing methods that attend to
gender as an expressive and behavioral phenomenon, drawing from recent
theorizing in Trans Studies as an example. This view is more in line
with conservative emphasis on gender as a physical phenomenon, though
without the ultimately fascist interests in establishing biological
boundaries and requirements.

The second paper, "'My Body Feels Like it’s Coming Off': on Cis
Embodiment in \emph{Love Is Blind}," currently under review at \emph{TSQ: Trans
Studies Quarterly}, examines what cisgender experiences of embodiment
can lend to Trans Studies understanding of body dysphoria. It analyzes
a dating reality TV show, \emph{Love Is Blind}, which places its
participants in a dating experiment that prevents them from seeing
their partner until they have agreed to get married. The paper argues
that the dating environment, where visual access to the beloved is
suppressed, simultaneously amplifies the participants' relationships
to their other bodily senses, particularly the sense of touch. This
intensification of bodily sensation is disrupted when the participants
finally meet in person, and many of them cannot transplant their
visceral emotional connection into a physical relationship. The paper
argues that this experience, although within a firmly cisgender and
normative context, evokes aspects of Trans Studies theorizing of
dysphoria. It therefore opens a vector of commonality between actually
divergent life experiences.

\subsection{reading computational mechanism as a heuristic for cultural norms}
\label{sec:org360754e}

This research theme is the main argument of my forthcoming book,
\emph{Queer Code: Re-working Language Technologies}, which is in contract
with Bloomsbury Academic Press.

The book explores how language technologies reduce language complexity
to make it computable. Words are removed from their original context,
cleaned and standardized, so that they can be stored and analyzed at
scale. These increasingly automated processes encode dominant logics
and extractive incentives onto language data, creating well-documented
issues with data sovereignty, privacy, and most recently, bias, as
current AI tools demonstrate.

Each of the book's five chapters considers how these processes of
reduction operate through different language technologies, such as web
scraping or text analysis. Each chapter identifies a mechanism in each
technology that reduces or flattens language meaning. These
mechanisms, which the book calls "technical constraints," are specific
to the technology in question---for example, web scraping exhibits the
technical constraint of protocol, while text analysis exhibits the
technical constraint of iteration. Then, drawing from Queer Studies
and related fields, the book identifies conceptual tools that re-work
these constraints into new analytical methodologies to open up novel
readings of language data.

tool across each of the book's five chapters.

For example, chapter 2, "Text Analysis," explores the technology of
\emph{quantitative text analysis} and its constraint of \emph{iteration}: a
programmatic logic of repetition in which text analysis programs
execute the same action across large collections of text, one word at
a time. This constraint cleans and regularizes texts en masse, with
the attendant effect of supressing minor details in language forms in
favor of more frequent textual patterns. The chapter then takes Judith
Butler's concept of \emph{Gender Performativity} as a conceptual tool to
re-work iteration into a new text analysis practice for studying
gender in language.

In addition, my article, "Re-encoding Dominance: Queer Approaches To
TEI Markup/, published in the book collection, \emph{Digital Editing \&
Publishing in the 21st Century}, explores the language technology, TEI
(Text Encoding Initiative), a markup standard for encoding text-based
cultural heritage materials. It finds that TEI imposes a constraint of
"containment," where all elements must be bounded and fixed as static
elements within an ordered hierarchy. The article thinks through how
this technical constraint reflects other power structures that operate
upon a text, like those implicit in editorial practices, literary
standards, and censorship.

\subsection{communicating across the disciplines}
\label{sec:orge4e431c}

Disciplinary and field boundaries are useful for organizing knowledge,
education, and training. But the fact is that most reseach projects
cross these boundaries in some way. In my research, however,
boundary-crossing is more than a fact, it is an aspiration: to engage
knowledges that otherwise may not come into contact with one another.

For my work, this means bringing knowledge from one academic domain
into another. In the past two years, I have written three papers (one
published and two under review) for primarily Computer Science fields
like Computational Linguistics and Machine Learning/AI Ethics. In
turn, I have written two papers (one published, one under review) for
the Humanities fields of Trans Studies and Digital Scholarly Editing.

On the side of Computer Science, my papers include, "Some Myths About
Bias: A Queer Studies Reading Of Gender Bias In NLP," published in the
\emph{Association for Computational Linguistics 2025 Proceedings}, "What
LLMs Can't See About Gender," currently under review at \emph{FAccT 2027},
and "Small Language Models and the Synthetic Middle," currently under
review at \emph{NeurIPS 2026}. These papers draw from theorizing in Queer
Studies and Trans Studies in order to make recommendations to CS
fields. For example, "Some Myths About Bias" paper argues that bias
mitigation techniques may perpetuate certain myths about gender bias:
first, that bias is a categorical value, where detection is equivalent
to evaluation and determination of harm; second, that bias is
zero-sum, that it can be "deducted" language in a way that achieves
equality between the genders. Rather, the paper draws from Queer
Studies theorizing to argue that bias is embedded in language in ways
that require more careful and thorough handling, without falling into
the trap of binary thinking. Additionally, my paper "Whatt LLMs Can't
See About Gender," shows how language systems will learn to
discriminate against genders that are not explicitly marked as such.
Drawing from the Humanities field of Trans Studies, the paper points
out that theorizing gender as an internal identity harms those whose
gender presentation is most visibly non-normative: the paper explains
that when gender is defined as internal, genders which are apparently
normative will be read as "default", while non-normative genders
become hyper-visible and therefore vulnerable to stigmatization.
Finally, my paper, "Small Language Models," under review at \emph{NeurIPS
2026}, is motivated by an interdisciplinary goal: to incorporate the
humanities analytical method of "close reading" into a larger
quantitative experiment. This paper uses close reading to develop a
novel methodology that uses Small Language Models (SLMs) to study
polarized discourses on gender and transgender rights in the USA.

On the side of the Humanities, I have written two papers, one in the
field of Digital Scholarly Editing and another in Trans Studies. The
former paper, "Re-encoding dominance," considers how Queer Studies's
concepts might help to rethink the rigidity of TEI, a text encoding
language for preserving and displaying cultural and literary material
in electronic formats. It considers how archival work in the history
of slavery offers lessons for working within rigid encoding systems
like the TEI---strategies like narrativizing between the gaps of what,
in Saidiya Harman's words, "cannot be verified" (Hartman, "Venus In
Two Acts", 12). The second paper, "My Body Feels Like It’s Coming
Off," applies the humanist methodology of close reading to results
from a machine learning experiment. For the paper, I fine-tuned a
Small Language Model (SLM) on transcipts from the reality TV dating
show, \emph{Love Is Blind}. Then I apply a framework from Trans Studeis to
read and analyzes the model outputs. The goal of this paper is to
incorporate computer science methods into a humanist methodology of
close reading in a way that is legible to those trained in the
humanities. 

\subsection{bank}
\label{sec:org82273ad}
While my formal training is in Literary Studies, Queer Studies, and
the Digital Humanities, I am a self-taught programmer who takes
computational tools both as a methodology for analyzing language.
Historically, this has meant applying a critical Humanist perspective
to analyze how computational tools, like text analysis and text
encoding for example, transform literary language into computable
data. I was interested in what happens to language meaning, conveyed
in formal structures and devices, like narrative form or metaphor,
when it is digitized, computed, and stored in electronic formats.

Although \emph{Queer Code} is written for Digital Humanities (DH) and
particularly Queer DH, its scaffolded approach to technology also aims
at nontechnical readerships from the Humanities, particularly from
Queer Studies and Literary Studies. First, Counter-Code will appeal to
readers from DH and Queer DH who are interested in learning how
theoretical frameworks like Queer Studies (including the debates and
trajectories within the field) apply to digital work. Second, the
book’s focus on Queer Studies and Literary Studies will appeal to
readers from those backgrounds, who may be nontechnical although
interested in applying close reading methodologies to digital
contexts. The book’s scaffolded approach to language technology as a
stack of increasingly complex mechanisms, from text capture to text
generation, make technical processes intelligible to readers without
formal training.
