1. TODO Mejias, Ulises A., and Nick Couldry. Data Grab: The New Colonialism of Big Tech and How to Fight Back. University of Chicago Press, 2024. University of Chicago Press, https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/D/bo216184200.html.

Argues that colonialism has moved from land to data; from the seizure and management of land and extraction of its resources to the capture, processing, and monetization of data. This new order of data colonialism takes the same colonial moves (such as exploration, exploitation, and extermination) and applies them within a new territory, which is society, and a new object, which is data. Society's behavior patterns are captured in the form of data, then quantified, analyzed, and predicted for the purpose of monetization and surveillance (also a form of monetization). There are possibilities of resistence from various standpoints, from within, against, and beyond the system.

From historical colonialism to data colonialism:

  • Defines colonialism as a means of extraction and capitalism as a means of production; argues that historical colonialism has moved into capitalism (29).
    • "Data colonialism introduces new forms of inequality connected to the exploitation of data, which often replay and deepen those older inequalities, including racial and gender discrimination" (Meijas & Couldry 19).
  • from land to data
    • "Once colonies were established, a system was put in place for the continuous extraction of resources from these territories, and for the transformation of these resources into riches. Big Tech has achieved a similar feat of exploitation by setting up business models that convert ‘our’data – that is, data resulting from tracking our lives and those of others – into wealth and power for them" (Meijas & Couldry 6).
      • "This is possible thanks to are arrangement of many aspects of our daily life in such a way that ensures we are continuously generating data" (Meijas & Couldry 6).
    • comparing Chrome's TOS to the Requerimiento, "a document read by the Spanish conquistadors as they entered a village or city in the Americas in the middle of the night to wreak havoc and get their pillaging under way. They read this document in Spanish… to indigenous or First Nation peoples who did not speak Spanish" (Meijas & Couldry 13).
      • "Incomprehensible language must be used, because extraction requires that we don’t fully understand the terms under which it is being carried out" (Meijas & Couldry 14).
  • Taking what is there:
    • "both systems are founded on an appropriation of the world’s resources, treating them as ‘just there’ for the taking. Historically it was the territories, natural resources and labour of the Americas, Africa and much of Asia and the Pacific that were plundered. In the contemporary era, it is human life, extracted in the form of data, that is being appropriated" (Meijas & Couldry 18).
  • Violence, Civilizing missions
    • Whereas colonialism required violence from the outset, capitalism works through consent which is in some cases implict:
      • "Historic colonialism is brutal and inhumane (Meijas & Couldry 34).
      • "no violence is needed to persuade you to click the box that says ‘I agree to the terms and conditions’ before installing an app… today’s forms of extraction are almost frictionless, although that doesn’t mean their long-term repercussions are entirely non-violent" (Meijas & Couldry 35).
    • "the predatory acts of colonialism are so manifestly unjust that they have always had to be disguised by more positive ‘civilising’ narratives, or ‘alibis’. In past centuries, those narratives were tied to Eurocentric notions of progress or Christian ideals of salvation. In the Big Tech era those narratives connect more closely to the values of a capitalist economy, while retaining echoes of earlier civilising missions. Can we imagine Facebook taking on the role of providing internet infrastructure to over thirty African countries without these sorts of narratives?" (Meijas & Couldry 19).
    • "Data colonialism’s process of relentless territorial expansioninto daily life often comes disguised in the form of apparent benefitsor even gifts" (Meijas and Couldry 70).
  • Platforms:
    • "In everyday life, we know those data territories generally as digital ‘platforms’" like Facebook and Amazon websites, or the Google search engine, "All of them give us access to a commercially controlled space from which data can be exclusively and continuously extracted from us" (Meijas & Couldry 60).
    • "Just as colonial territories imposed new laws on their inhabitants, so data territories exert their own ‘laws’ based on the code that keeps the platform running" (Meijas & Couldry 60).
    • Draws from Agre's concept of "grammar of action" ("computers do not just capture information in the abstract: they capture iton their own terms," imposing a "grammar that always includes the capture of data", and from Lessig's "Code is law" dictum, whereby "platform code… sets the law of data territories" (Meijas and Couldry 60).
    • "Almost the whole internet is now completely 'platformized'… You may notice this when big platforms act like gatekeepers to websites or smaller platforms, and you are asked to sign up via your Facebook or Google ID, or in China via your WeChat ID, ensuring that the data from you using that new platform is combined with yourFacebook, Google or WeChat data, to those corporations’ benefit" (Meijas and Couldry 74).
    • Happening across agriculture, education, health, "AgTech, EdTech and Health Tech" (Meijas and Couldry 90).

Possibilities of resistance:

  • Everyone is implicated:
    • "in data colonialim we are all informants. We literally trans-late our lives for the coloniser by conducting our social lives ontheir platforms" (Meijas & Couldry 54).
  • Potential failures of translation in commodification?
    • "A global network of scientists and practitioners worked in concert for the benefit of their employers or institutions. Much of this went on in the colonies. For example, between 1717 and 1738 Spain imported 45 tons a year of medicinal plants from the Americas, a number that increased to 155 tons a year between 1747 and 1778. The knowledge of how to use these plants came from indigenous peoples, but was systematised and commercialised by apothecary missionaries in the colonies, as the name of the popular fever-controlling plant of the time, cinchona, known as Jesuits’ bark in Europe, implies. By the end of the seventeenth century, these Big Sciences (medicinal botany, observational astronomy, geography, natural history, meteorology, navigation, etc.) had ‘modernised’ themselves into what today we would call distributed or virtual teams" (Meijas & Couldry 48).
  • capture is dysfunctional:
    • "This very act of seizing territory, without consent and without care for the consequences, indicates a dysfunctional relation to the world and a way of living with people that is extractive rather than reciprocal" (Meijas & Couldry 182).
  • "resistance means holding onto values… socially generated values" like knowledge as relation (Meijas & Couldry 184).
    • Drawing from the indigenous academic, writer, and musician, Leanne Betasamosake Simpson's book, As We Have Always Done (2020).
    • "On this social view of knowledge, each person must not only develop their own ideas, but share those ideas with others in a way that tries not to ‘interfere with other beings’ life pathways’, that is, in a way that does not impose her own understanding on others In this indigenous way of understanding ideas as what emerge in the context of living together on shared land and territory, a completely different understanding ofshared knowledgeemerges. Within this perspective, data colonialism’s drivingprinciple – that you can get knowledge by extracting patterns at a distance from myriad data sources across the planet, many of them accessed without consent and outside of rich humanrelationships – becomes both nonsensical and alarming" (Meijas & Couldry 183).
  • Gig workers unions, indigenous sovereignty movements, activism against tech (no tech for ICE/Apartheid), whistleblowers.
  • From Mijente, the three-pronged approach to resistance:
    • working within: spreading awareness, elections, regulation, taxation
    • working against: refusal, self-examination, bias exposure, changing curricula
    • working beyond: support/join initiatives that seek new data practices
